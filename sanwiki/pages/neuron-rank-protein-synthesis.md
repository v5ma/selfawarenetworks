**Neuron rank via protein synthesis** is [[gh-resnote04|Micah]] Blumberg]]'s mechanistic account of how neural patterns earn priority in the brain: each time a neuron fires, it triggers new protein synthesis, increasing the neuron's structural mass and electromagnetic density. More mass → stronger local electromagnetic weight → higher probability of future firing when the same pattern arrives — the neuron has gained "rank."

## Mechanism

1. A pattern arrives and a neuron fires (triggered by coincidence across enough upstream inputs — see [[coincidence-pattern-neural-bit]]).
2. Firing initiates protein synthesis, adding material to dendritic branches and synaptic structures.
3. Added protein = added structural mass = increased electromagnetic density at that synapse.
4. Higher electromagnetic density biases future activation: the neuron becomes more likely to fire again when similar partial patterns appear.
5. Concurrently, dendritic branching may expand, increasing the number of inputs the neuron integrates — further elevating rank.

This is a mass-based, density-centric reframing of [[long-term-potentiation|long-term potentiation (LTP)]].

## Role of neurotransmitters

Vesicle stacks inside the axon terminal hold dopamine, GABA, [[acetylcholine]], and other neurotransmitters. On firing, they are pushed into the synaptic cleft, reaching receptors on downstream neurons. The downstream effect (excitation or inhibition, timing of the next firing) shapes which downstream neurons rank up. [[adderall-dopamine-dynamics|Dopamine]] in particular modulates which patterns are selected for reinforcement.

## Pattern propagation consequence

Ranked patterns propagate more reliably through the [[neural-array-projection-oscillation-tomography|neural array hierarchy]]: each brain layer re-represents the pattern at a different temporal stage, effectively replaying it as a cascading sequence. Higher-rank neurons sustain this cascade better, making the pattern more memorable and more likely to contribute to long-range oscillations.
