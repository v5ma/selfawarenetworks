# Traveling Waves in Neural Oscillations

**Traveling waves** are spatially ordered changes in neural activity whose phase or amplitude pattern moves across tissue. A traveling wave is therefore more than a rhythm measured at one electrode: it requires spatial sampling that can distinguish propagation from a simultaneous global change, volume conduction, reference effects, and trial averaging.

## What has been measured

Human intracranial recordings have shown behaviorally relevant theta- and alpha-band traveling waves across contiguous cortical regions during memory tasks ([Zhang et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2018.05.019)). In macaque prefrontal cortex, Bhattacharya and colleagues found planar and rotating waves in theta, alpha, and beta bands during a working-memory task; direction and prevalence changed with task state ([PLOS Computational Biology, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1009827)). Touch-evoked recordings in rodent somatosensory cortex have also resolved translaminar waves with frequency- and latency-dependent structure ([Science Advances, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adr4038)).

These results establish multiple preparation-specific wave phenomena. They do not establish one universal direction, frequency, generator, or cognitive meaning.

## Waves, bursts, and working memory

Miller Lab results distinguish two related but non-identical observations. Lundqvist and colleagues measured brief beta and gamma bursts associated with working-memory encoding and reactivation rather than one continuously sustained oscillation ([Neuron, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2016.02.028)). Bhattacharya and colleagues later measured the spatial propagation of lower-frequency activity across PFC ([PLOS Computational Biology, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1009827)). A burst is a temporally bounded spectral event; a traveling wave is a spatial phase or amplitude pattern. Either can occur without proving the other.

The "Working Memory 2.0" synthesis treats working memory as dynamic interaction among spiking, synaptic state, and beta/gamma bursts rather than one fixed store ([Miller, Lundqvist, and Bastos, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2018.09.023)). This supports a dynamic-state comparison with SAN while leaving the SAN-specific receiver, rendering, and observer-action claims open.

Newer Miller Lab results add spatial and behavioral constraints. After distraction during a macaque working-memory task, recovery toward the pre-disruption population state corresponded with traveling waves across prefrontal cortex; more complete state-space rotations accompanied correct performance ([Batabyal et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1162/JOCN.a.2410)). In frontal eye field, working-memory readout varied with local theta phase and target location, consistent with but not by itself proving a retinotopically organized traveling-wave account ([Han et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2025.09.031)). Chen and colleagues found that task-dependent alpha/beta spatial patterns in macaque prefrontal cortex gated where content-related spiking was expressed ([Current Biology, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2025.11.072)). These findings support spatially organized oscillatory control and state-dependent readout. They do not establish tomographic reconstruction, a universal frequency code, or SAN's complete mechanism.

## Candidate mechanisms

Traveling waves can arise through several mechanisms, including gradients in intrinsic frequency or excitability, horizontal and laminar connectivity, conduction and synaptic delays, recurrent excitation and inhibition, external input, and boundary geometry. Which mechanism applies must be tested in the named circuit. A spatial phase gradient alone does not identify its generator.

Sharp-wave ripples are a separate hippocampal event class. They can coordinate replay and hippocampal-cortical interaction, but they should not be used as a generic synonym for cortical traveling waves.

## SAN interpretation

SAN proposes that some traveling waves may carry receiver-relative phase geometry through recurrent neural arrays. Under this interpretation, a candidate [[phase-wave-differentials|phase-wave differential]] must identify the source, propagation path, receiver population, state change at that receiver, and downstream consequence. A measured wave is relevant evidence for this mechanism, but is not automatically a PWD, a [[neural-array-projection-oscillation-tomography|NAPOT]] reconstruction, or conscious content.

NAPOT adds a stronger claim: multiple partial population states are integrated and redistributed to update a working model. Demonstrating that role requires more than observing motion across electrodes. The experiment must show what partial variables are combined, where integration occurs, how the result is re-expressed, which populations receive it, and what selective perturbation disrupts the proposed update.

## Measurement requirements

Report electrode geometry, reference, spatial sampling, frequency definition, propagation estimator, trial-level prevalence, direction, velocity, laminar location, and controls for evoked latency gradients and volume conduction. Compare traveling-wave models with stationary oscillation, common-input, local-signal, and topology-only alternatives. Then perturb the candidate route or generator and predict both the specific loss and rescue.
