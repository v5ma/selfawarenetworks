# Dendrites as Computational Units

**Dendritic computation** means that synaptic input is transformed within the dendritic arbor before its effects reach the soma and axon. Branch geometry, receptor kinetics, membrane voltage, voltage-gated channels, inhibition, neuromodulation, and recent activity can make that transformation nonlinear and compartment specific. This is more capable than a passive point-neuron sum, but it does not make every branch a Boolean gate or an independently conscious miniature neuron.

## Established cellular physiology

Passive cable properties attenuate and filter synaptic voltage according to distance, branch diameter, membrane resistance, capacitance, and branching. Active conductances can then amplify or reshape selected input configurations. Depending on cell type and compartment, dendrites can produce NMDA-receptor-dependent plateaus, sodium spikes, calcium spikes, and other regenerative events.

Nearby inputs on the same thin branch can combine supralinearly, while separated inputs can combine differently. [Polsky, Mel, and Schiller (2004)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1253) demonstrated branch-dependent nonlinear integration in rat neocortical pyramidal neurons. [Branco, Clark, and Hausser (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1189664) showed that input order and direction along a dendrite can change calcium signals, somatic depolarization, and spike output. These are defined forms of spatial-temporal discrimination, not evidence that one branch recognizes arbitrary semantic concepts.

Dendritic events can affect natural sensory processing. Visually evoked local dendritic spikes enhanced orientation selectivity in mouse V1 in [Smith et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature12600). Sensory-evoked NMDA spikes in layer 2/3 tuft dendrites substantially influenced axonal output in [Palmer et al. (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.3646). These results establish causal contributions in particular preparations, not one universal cortical algorithm.

Active dendrites can also affect behavior and learning in bounded preparations. Manipulating apical-dendritic calcium activity shifted whisker-detection thresholds in mouse primary somatosensory cortex ([Takahashi et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aah6066)). Different motor-learning tasks recruited branch-specific calcium spikes and plasticity in apical tufts of mouse motor-cortex Layer 5 pyramidal neurons ([Cichon and Gan, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature14251)). These experiments do not establish that an isolated branch contains a percept, semantic choice, or complete memory.

## One neuron can contain multiple interacting computational domains

A useful abstraction treats a neuron as a two-stage or multi-compartment system:

1. branch and dendritic domains transform local input relations;
2. somatic and axon-initial-segment dynamics integrate those distributed effects into graded voltage, spikes, or bursts.

The soma does not inspect branch decisions. Voltage and current propagate physically through the cell, and many local events remain local or primarily alter plasticity. Output commonly begins at the axon initial segment in cortical pyramidal cells, while backpropagating action potentials return information about output into dendritic compartments.

Inhibition is part of the computation rather than a scalar vote against excitation. Its timing, location, receptor type, reversal potential, and circuit source can veto local electrogenesis, shorten a plateau, alter backpropagation, separate branch activity, or produce disinhibition.

## Plasticity changes future transformations

Synaptic efficacy, receptor composition, spine structure, intrinsic excitability, and inhibition can change after activity. A dendritic branch can therefore become differentially prepared for a later input relation. This supports SAN's language of **changed readiness**, provided the proposed criterion is tied to measurable input, dendritic state, output, and counterfactual alternatives.

The learned state is not automatically a complete memory or semantic token. Its significance depends on how recurrent populations reinstate and use it.

Human dendrites also warn against one universal branch-to-soma diagram. Direct recordings from human Layer 5 pyramidal neurons found stronger electrical compartmentalization than in rat neurons; distal dendritic spikes could remain weakly coupled to somatic output ([Beaulieu-Laroche et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2018.08.045)). Species, cell type, branch geometry, and state therefore belong in any medical interpretation.

## SAN and NAPOT interpretation

At the **micro scale**, SAN treats the dendritic arbor as the receptive and locally transforming side of a receive-integrate-project motif. A branch-resolved input relation changes cellular output or plasticity.

At the **meso scale**, recurrent excitation and inhibition select and stabilize population activity. At the **macro scale**, distributed sensory, association, thalamic, hippocampal, bodily, and motor systems reconstruct an actionable model.

NAPOT is therefore not tomography performed by one dendrite. The proposed tomography emerges when many cellular projections are gathered, transformed, redistributed, and compared through recurrent network activity. The network itself performs the observer-action process; no soma or hidden viewer reads a private image.

### What the rejected figure was trying to communicate

The eight legacy versions tried to contrast a biological pyramidal neuron with a point-neuron perceptron. They showed local spine patterns interacting with branch voltage, receptors, inhibition, and modulators before cell-wide output. That core contrast is defensible. The medically inaccurate parts were the fixed sequence *branch gate -> branch decision -> result sent inward -> soma decision*, the implication that every branch emits one discrete result, and the assignment of apical dendrites to context and basal dendrites to sensory evidence as a universal rule.

### Medically accurate replacement mechanism

A corrected figure should show several possible transformations rather than one mandatory pipeline:

1. passive attenuation and temporal filtering;
2. sublinear integration or shunting inhibition;
3. clustered-input NMDA plateaus or local sodium/calcium spikes;
4. sensitivity to input order, direction, and recent membrane state;
5. variable propagation toward the soma and axon initial segment;
6. backpropagating action potentials and local plasticity; and
7. possible cell outputs including no spike, graded depolarization, a spike, or a burst.

Apical, oblique, and basal compartments may receive different pathway mixtures in particular cell types and cortical areas, but *apical = prediction* and *basal = evidence* is not a universal anatomical identity.

### Distinctive SAN hypothesis recovered from the failed figure

SAN source notes use dendritic computation to propose a stronger receive-transform-project motif: learned receptor, spine, branch, inhibitory, and voltage states create a receiver-specific readiness; incoming relations transform that state; and any resulting change in cellular output or plasticity becomes one contribution to later recurrent population reconstruction.

The source also explores a basal-recognition/apical-response separation. That should remain explicitly labeled a SAN thought experiment, not established pyramidal-cell anatomy. Its testable form asks whether pathway-specific learned states in different dendritic compartments predict subsequent recognition, action selection, and recurrent recruitment beyond models based only on somatic firing.

## Testable boundary

A strong experiment should ask whether branch-resolved location, order, membrane state, and inhibition predict output or plasticity beyond a matched point-neuron model. A SAN-specific extension must additionally show how those local variables improve prediction of recurrent population reconstruction, receiver state, and behavior.
