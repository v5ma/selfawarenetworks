When multiple synaptic vesicles release neurotransmitters simultaneously, the resulting spatial distribution of neurotransmitter molecules across the synaptic cleft may encode **positional/spatial information**, not merely a binary on/off signal. This is the multi-vesicle release spatial encoding hypothesis in the SAN framework.

## Key claims

- **More material, wider spread**: Multiple vesicle releases dramatically increase the concentration and spatial extent of neurotransmitter in the synaptic region, allowing downstream neurons to detect *where* the signal came from, not just *that* it arrived.
- **Calcium channel gating**: Delayed potassium activation extends the calcium channel open time, increasing the probability and magnitude of multi-vesicle release. [[gh-a0138zcalcium|Calcium dynamics]] are therefore a gate on the richness of spatial encoding.
- **Ion channel power law**: Sodium produces positive spikes; potassium produces negative ones. Across scales (single neuron to circuit), frequency and amplitude obey an inverse power law — high-frequency bursts carry low amplitude and vice versa. This scale-invariance is consistent with [[fractal-consciousness]].
- **19-axon anomaly**: Observed 19 axon connections rather than the canonical single-axon connection at a synapse — raising the possibility of previously uncharacterized multi-pathway spatial signaling.

## Relation to SAN theory

Standard neuroscience frames synaptic transmission as a scalar signal (amount of neurotransmitter equals signal strength). SAN theory argues information is richer: the *spatial pattern* of neurotransmitter spread across the cleft can represent a vector, mapping onto [[napot]]'s picture of neural arrays that project structured patterns rather than scalar values.

Multi-vesicle spatial encoding is one physical mechanism that could instantiate [[memory-as-probability-distribution]]: different release geometries create different probability landscapes for downstream dendritic integration.
