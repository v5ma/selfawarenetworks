Standard [[neuroimaging]] studies that correlate brain activity patterns with psychological constructs (personality disorders, behavioral traits) face a replication crisis: when researchers retest, the same neural correlates are often absent. This failure motivates the SAN approach of studying *mechanisms* rather than *correlates*.

## The critique

[[gh-resnote04|Micah]] Blumberg]]'s position: psychological labels like "narcissist" are subjective characterizations, not hard medical science grounded in provable neural correlates. Duke University researchers (ca. 2020) confirmed that a large fraction of published brain activity studies cannot be replicated — their neural correlates vanish on retest.

The analogy offered: "trying to find a personality disorder in brain activity is like trying to find a file on your computer by reading the heat coming from your hard drive." The signal is too indirect and too noisy to index discrete cognitive or personality constructs.

## Why this matters for SAN

[[self-aware-networks]] theory sidesteps the correlation trap by targeting the *rendering mechanism* directly — how the brain constructs the conscious experience of reality — rather than mapping behavioral labels onto activation patterns. This requires:

- Understanding [[cortical-column-signal-projection]] and [[oscillatory-synchrony]] as the computational substrate
- Accepting that memories and percepts are sparse and distributed across many brain regions simultaneously
- Treating medical imaging as a tool for revealing constraints on the mechanism, not for localizing psychological constructs

## Distributed sparse representations

Conscious experience involves networks and memories that are "sparse and distributed all over a network, involving sensory areas of the brain and all sorts of brain regions even in small tasks." Localizationist assumptions built into standard fMRI analysis violate this architecture.
