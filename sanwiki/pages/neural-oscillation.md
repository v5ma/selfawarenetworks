A **neural oscillation** is a recurring or approximately rhythmic change in neural activity. It can be observed in membrane voltage, synaptic current, spiking probability, local field potential, intracranial EEG, scalp EEG, or MEG. These signals sample different biological scales and cannot be treated as interchangeable views of one wave.

## How rhythms arise

Rhythms can emerge from intrinsic membrane conductances, recurrent excitation and inhibition, pacemaker circuits, delayed feedback, sensory entrainment, movement, or interactions among populations. Some are sustained; many occur as brief bursts or changing traveling patterns. A spectral bump is therefore not proof of a stable oscillator.

Llinas's 1988 synthesis distinguished cellular oscillators or pacemakers from resonators whose voltage-dependent conductances make them respond preferentially to particular input frequencies ([Science, 1988](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.3059497)). This is a cell- and conductance-level result. It does not imply that every neuron has one intrinsic clock, that one frequency identifies represented content, or that an intrinsic cellular rhythm alone explains a population rhythm.

Frequency-band names are descriptive conveniences. Alpha, beta, gamma, theta, and slower activity do not define fixed cognitive departments. Their ranges and functions vary with circuit, task, species, and method.

## Oscillation is not a complete code

Power, phase, frequency, waveform, coherence, phase-amplitude coupling, and traveling-wave direction are distinct variables. To call one a neural code, an account must identify a source, route, receiver, decoding operation, causal use, and failure condition. A pattern that an external analyst can decode may not be available to a biological receiver.

## SAN interpretation

SAN treats ongoing activity as a potential reference context and event-related differences as potential updates. [[phase-wave-differentials|Phase-wave differentials]] name the stronger receiver-relative hypothesis; [[neural-array-projection-oscillation-tomography|NAPOT]] names a proposed cross-population reconstruction cycle. Neither follows from detecting a rhythm.

The tonic canvas and phasic ink are SAN's proposed operational roles, expressed through a visual analogy. The tonic population state supplies an ongoing receiver context; relatively sparse phasic departures carry differentiated updates that alter that context or its downstream consequences. These roles are hypotheses about measurable state dependence and causal use, not claims that a literal screen exists. The participating field-cell-network operation performs the observing action; no separate localized viewer is added.

## Measurement burden

Declare the signal, reference, spatial scale, temporal window, spectral method, source, receiver, task, artifacts, and matched alternatives. Demonstrate incremental prediction and a selective perturbation before assigning a computational role.

## Primary anchors

- Llinas distinguished conductance-based cellular oscillators or pacemakers from frequency-selective resonators ([Science, 1988](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.3059497)).
- Bastos et al. found distinct frequency profiles for directed feedforward and feedback influences in macaque visual cortex, a pathway-specific result rather than a universal band anatomy ([Neuron, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2014.12.018)).
- Ray and Maunsell showed why rhythmic gamma and broadband high-frequency activity must be separated ([PLOS Biology, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.1000610)).
