# The Transcendent Human

The source note imagines a moment of realizing that the mind may be understood as a computational program. In that framing, perceived reality exists both as the outside world and as a rendered three-dimensional perspective built by the brain.

The author describes only the needed parts of the perspective as being rendered. In this speculative model, each component of the rendering is compared to a tiny wavelet or soliton-like element that contributes to the canvas of the mind. The note connects this imagined element to an [action potential](action-potential.md), treating neural firing as a possible carrier for temporal and spatial codes that downstream neurons could listen for and recognize.

The page then extends the model to cortical columns. It proposes that columns may alternate between listening and firing phases, and that groups of neurons might share firing instructions that help reproduce higher-level patterns. The claim is presented here as the source author's hypothesis, not as an established neuroscience result.

The author also imagines each perceived visual pixel as a short-lived frequency-like event in a multi-sensory field of awareness. This passage frames conscious perception as a rapidly refreshed internal canvas rather than as a direct copy of the outside world.

The source includes a personal exercise: while crossing the eyes, the author reports being able to imagine simple shapes such as a black letter W, a black letter X, or a cross. The note interprets this experience as a way of consciously bending the brain's own rendered pixels, using the film image of bending the spoon in The Matrix as a metaphor.

The exercise asks the reader to cross their eyes, think of vision as a computational rendering, and imagine a shape in the internal representation. The source uses this exercise to argue for a computational view of the mind.

The note then compares the mind to a simulation engine, a holodeck, or a game engine. In that analogy, the brain renders a virtualized camera or viewpoint from incoming sensory data and from the stereo positions of the eyes.

The source also references Gyorgy Buzsaki's discussion of rats and brain rhythms in *Rhythms of the Brain*. The author uses that reference to speculate that animals may inherit predispositions toward certain modes of thought, and that learning reshapes those inherited blocks over a lifetime.

The final civic passage shifts from perception to public deliberation. It asks why societies do not hold more public discussions between people who support political leaders and people who dislike them, with civil discourse, public fact checking, and social media used to expose disagreements to wider review.

The note closes with the phrase, "The consciousness without the content."
