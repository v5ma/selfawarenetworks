# Stimulus History and Action-Potential Waveform

An action potential is regenerative and often described as **all-or-none**, but that principle does
not require every spike waveform or every synaptic consequence to be identical.

> **Scientific boundary:** Variable waveform is established in specific preparations. A universal rule that recognition, novelty, surprise, whisker touch, or umami always broadens action potentials is not established.

Spike peak, width, rise, repolarization, afterpotentials, burst position, and propagation can vary
with cell type, compartment, temperature, conductances, neuromodulation, and recent input history.

*Figure: Population-level `1/f` power is separated from cellular action-potential duration. Recent
input history may shape spike waveform, but downstream calcium, release, receiver response, and
population effects are conditionally transformed variables. Whisker, umami, and novelty paradigms
are proposed tests, not assumed universal broadening effects.*

## The established result

De Polavieja and colleagues drove rat visual-cortex pyramidal neurons with dynamic conductance input
designed to resemble natural synaptic activity. Somatic spike height and width reliably carried
information about the preceding stimulus history for up to about 50 ms. In that preparation, the
waveform carried information not captured by instantaneous firing rate alone. See
[de Polavieja et al. (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0242-05.2005).

This is evidence that a spike can be a richer physical event than a binary pulse. It does not show
that the complete stimulus, memory, or percept is contained in one spike.

## Recovered intent

The source-faithful claim is not that every recognized or surprising stimulus makes every action
potential wider. The stronger and more testable SAN proposal is:

> A learned receptor and synaptic configuration, together with recent input and membrane state, may produce a multidimensional spike-or-burst signature. That signature matters only to the extent that it survives or is transformed through identified boutons, synapses, receivers, recurrent populations, and behavior.

Recognition, novelty, and surprise are therefore candidate **conditions to classify**, not synonyms
for broadening. A useful signature could involve width, height, afterpotential, burst position,
propagation, terminal calcium, or release statistics, and different cell types or pathways may use
different combinations. The hypothesis gains support only when such variables identify the
condition beyond stimulus energy, movement, arousal, membrane state, firing rate, and spike timing.

## The receiver transformation

A source waveform matters downstream only through biological transformations:

1. the waveform reaching an identified axonal branch or bouton;
2. voltage-gated calcium-channel activation and local calcium;
3. active-zone geometry, vesicle pools, and stochastic fusion;
4. transmitter clearance and transmitter-matched receptors;
5. postsynaptic voltage, dendritic location, inhibition, and recent state;
6. recurrent population and behavioral consequences.

Somatic width is therefore not automatically terminal width, vesicle count, postsynaptic-current
duration, population persistence, or subjective intensity.

## Three candidate sensory laboratories

### Whisker touch

Active whisking changes membrane-potential dynamics and sensory sampling in barrel cortex. Quiet and
whisking states differ in fluctuation amplitude, speed, correlation, and contact response. See
[Crochet and Petersen (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1690). The open question is whether
contact identity, novelty, or learned recognition produces a waveform contribution after membrane
state, cell type, rate, timing, and movement are controlled.

### Umami

Umami begins with chemical and receptor-specific transduction, not a cortical label. Multiple taste
receptors and cell pathways can contribute to related taste responses. A SAN experiment should
measure receptor activation, intracellular state, conductance, cellular output, afferent activity,
and receiver response together. It should not infer action-potential broadening from receptor
activation without recording it.

### Novelty and surprise

Surprise is a relation between an event and an organism's expectation. It can alter attention,
neuromodulation, membrane state, firing probability, latency, bursting, adaptation, and population
dynamics. Whether waveform duration adds an independent variable is an empirical question.

## `1/f` is a population-spectrum observation

The aperiodic component of EEG, MEG, ECoG, and local-field power spectra is often approximated by

`P(f) = C f^(-beta)`, with `C > 0` and `beta > 0` over the fitted frequency range.

This means that power tends to decline with increasing frequency over a specified range. It does
not mean that individual spike amplitude is inversely proportional to spike frequency or duration.
A bridge from cellular waveform distributions to population spectra requires a forward model and
simultaneous cellular and field measurements.

## Bounded SAN hypothesis

SAN proposes that a neural event can be a receiver-relative, multidimensional departure from an
ongoing tonic state. Applied here:

> Recent receptor, synaptic, and membrane history may alter a spike or burst waveform, whose transformed bouton and receiver consequences contribute information beyond spike occurrence, count, and timing.

The strongest experiment compares expected, recognized, and novel inputs while matching stimulus
energy, movement, firing rate, spike timing, cell identity, and baseline state. It records soma,
axon or bouton, calcium, release, receiver response, population state, and behavior. The waveform
hypothesis is supported only if waveform variables improve held-out prediction and selective
waveform manipulation changes the predicted receiver or behavioral outcome. It is weakened if rate,
timing, membrane state, adaptation, or known synaptic mediators explain the result.
