**SPEAR** (Separate Phases at Encoding And Retrieval) is a neuroscience hypothesis
proposing that the [[hippocampus]] uses distinct oscillatory phase windows within its
[[theta-oscillation]] cycle to prevent interference between encoding new memories and
retrieving old ones.

## Core mechanism

During the theta cycle (~4–8 Hz), the hippocampus alternates between an *encoding
phase* (when novel input is written into synaptic weights) and a *retrieval phase*
(when stored patterns are reactivated). The phase offset acts as a temporal gate —
new information cannot overwrite an active retrieval trace, and retrieval does not
contaminate incomplete encoding.

## Relevance to SAN theory

SPEAR maps directly onto [[napot]]'s claim that [[self-aware-network]] activity
separates temporal contexts via oscillatory timing. The same phase-gating logic
that prevents memory interference in the hippocampus appears at scale in
[[neomindcycle]] dynamics: global cortical cycles demarcate "write" from "read"
windows in the [[fractal-consciousness]] hierarchy.

Hye Bin Yoo et al. (2021) showed that neurons in the medial temporal lobe track
*multiple* temporal contexts simultaneously during episodic memory processing —
consistent with the SAN view that a single neural assembly can hold layered
representations gated by phase rather than by distinct cell populations.

## History

The phase-offset hypothesis dates to Hasselmo et al. (2002), formalised as SPEAR.
It was later connected to quantum-scale signalling in speculative SAN notes
alongside work on stem-cell mechanosensing (density-sensing mechanical waves as
an analogy for how neurons sense population activity).
