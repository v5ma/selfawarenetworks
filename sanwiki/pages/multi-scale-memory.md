# Multi-Scale Memory

**Multi-scale memory** names a problem and a proposed causal bridge: how local,
experience-dependent changes at synapses and dendrites can help a partial cue recruit a learned
cell assembly, reinstate activity across connected brain regions, and influence present perception
or action.

> **Medical boundary:** A memory is not known to exist as six identical copies at molecular,
> synaptic, cellular, columnar, hippocampal, and whole-brain scales. Molecular and synaptic
> plasticity, cellular allocation, ensemble reactivation, hippocampal-cortical interaction, and
> distributed retrieval are different processes measured with different methods. Coordination
> between some of those processes does not establish one universal frequency, phase relation,
> cortical-column circuit, or brain-wide coherence state.

## The source-faithful SAN question

In public SAN explanations from July 2022, Micah Blumberg repeatedly asked how a memory associated
with microscopic synaptic changes could become available to the activity of the wider brain. The
recorded formulation was that memory stored in synapses must be "magnified" or must "scale up" into
whole-brain-accessible activity. The same July 31 explanation added a group-completion proposal:
memory belongs to a learned oscillating group, so a partial cue received by one available member
can help recruit the rest even when another member is inhibited.

Read as a biological operation rather than a literal enlargement, the recoverable chain is:

`local synaptic and dendritic change -> altered cellular response probability -> cue-driven
ensemble recruitment -> recurrent pattern completion -> cross-regional reinstatement ->
memory-guided perception or action`

The key claim is therefore **causal re-expression across scales**, not that one physical code is
copied unchanged into every scale.

## Established evidence at different scales

### Synapses and dendritic spines

Learning can produce task-related changes in selected synapses and dendritic spines. In mouse motor
cortex, a probe that labelled recently potentiated spines identified a task-specific synaptic
ensemble; selectively shrinking those labelled spines disrupted the acquired motor skill
([Hayashi-Takagi et al., 2015](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature15257)).

This supports a causal role for selected synaptic changes in one task and preparation. It does not
show that a complete episodic memory, conscious image, or oscillatory waveform is stored inside one
spine.

Synapse-specific plasticity can also help separate overlapping memories. In mice, the identity of
overlapping memory engrams depended on specific connections between participating ensembles rather
than neuronal overlap alone
([Abdou et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aat3810)).

### Cells and local ensembles

Memory experiments often identify sparse populations whose activation during learning and
reactivation during retrieval are causally related to later behavior. These are functional
**engram-cell ensembles**, not a new anatomical cell type. Partial-cue completion can emerge from
recurrent connectivity and learned excitability within a specified circuit.

This scale is the closest established comparator for SAN's 2022 "oscillating group" proposal.
However, ordinary engram or attractor completion does not by itself establish SAN's phase-wave,
rendering, or consciousness interpretation.

### Hippocampal-cortical systems

Memory representations change across time and brain systems. Mouse studies of systems
consolidation have identified distinct hippocampal and prefrontal engram populations and circuits
whose causal roles change between recent and remote memory
([Kitamura et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28490776/)).

This is not a simple transfer of one file from hippocampus to cortex. Encoding, consolidation,
retrieval, and reconsolidation can involve interacting and partly reorganized traces.

### Distributed brain networks

A single learned event can recruit engram-related populations in multiple connected brain regions.
Brain-wide mapping and causal activation in mice support a distributed engram complex rather than
one storage site
([Roy et al., 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-29384-4);
[Vetere et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28426969/)).

Distributed does not mean that every active region contains the same code or that the entire brain
becomes phase-synchronous. Different regions can represent different features, contexts, actions,
or control variables.

## Oscillatory coordination: specific, not universal

Oscillatory relations can coordinate memory processing in particular pathways and tasks:

- Rat entorhinal and hippocampal ensembles developed 20-40 Hz coupling during acquisition and
  retrieval of an odor-place association
  ([Igarashi et al., 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature13162)).
- Intracranial recordings from human epilepsy patients found distinct hippocampal and lateral
  temporal reinstatement patterns whose trial-by-trial coordination was related to gamma phase
  synchronization during episodic retrieval
  ([Kunz et al., 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-09569-0)).
- Inter-regional ensemble coactivations can reappear during recall and occur with fast
  oscillations in several regions
  ([Ghandour et al., 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-28929-x)).

These results support pathway-, task-, species-, and state-dependent coordination. They do not
show a single sinusoid linking molecules, spines, neurons, columns, hippocampus, and the whole
brain. A molecular stabilization process does not possess an EEG phase merely because both affect
memory.

## SAN interpretation: scaling as recruitment and reconstruction

The medically bounded SAN proposal is:

> **SAN hypothesis:** Learning alters local receiver states and effective routes. A later cue
> activates a subset of those altered elements. Recurrent excitation, inhibition, pattern
> completion, and anatomically available long-range interactions then reconstruct a larger
> distributed state. Oscillatory timing may help select and coordinate some routes, while
> structured phase separation or inhibition may keep competing content distinct.

This preserves four useful SAN ideas:

1. microscopic plasticity matters because it changes later network recruitment;
2. the functional memory is an interacting pattern, not one privileged cell or inner viewer;
3. retrieval is an active reconstruction that can reach sensory, associative, emotional, and
   motor systems; and
4. both coupling and separation can be computationally meaningful.

The word **fractal** should be reserved for a measured scaling relation, such as self-similarity or
a power law across defined ranges. A hierarchy that merely contains several spatial or temporal
levels is multi-scale, but not automatically fractal.

## Micro, meso, and macro levels

| Level | Defensible biological description | What must not be inferred |
|---|---|---|
| Micro | Molecular, receptor, synaptic-strength, spine, branch, and intrinsic-excitability changes alter later responses. | One molecule, spine, or branch stores and displays the complete memory. |
| Meso | Learned cellular ensembles and recurrent local circuits support allocation, competition, cue completion, and reactivation. | One generic cortical column is a universal memory processor or conscious display. |
| Macro | Hippocampal, thalamic, cortical, subcortical, and motor networks can reinstate distributed, task-dependent activity. | Every region carries identical content or enters one globally coherent phase. |

The distributed network itself performs the retrieval and uses the result. No additional inner
observer is required.

## Bounded tests

1. **Cross-scale prediction:** identify learning-related synaptic or dendritic changes and test
   whether they improve prediction of later ensemble recruitment and behavior beyond firing rate
   and anatomy alone.
2. **Causal propagation:** perturb the identified local trace while measuring local ensemble,
   hippocampal-cortical, and behavioral consequences of the same retrieval cue.
3. **Content specificity:** test whether the perturbation changes the targeted memory while
   sparing a matched memory represented in overlapping cells or regions.
4. **Oscillatory increment:** compare models using firing, anatomy, and task state with models that
   additionally use pathway-specific phase, coherence, cross-frequency coupling, and structured
   dispersion.
5. **Matched-activity test:** alter the proposed timing relation while matching mean firing and
   overall power as closely as possible.
6. **Falsifier:** narrow the SAN scaling claim if local trace variables do not predict or cause
   later content-specific recruitment, or if oscillatory variables add no explanatory power beyond
   established anatomical and firing-rate models.

## Source genealogy

- In the [July 28, 2022 public SAN Institute introduction](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VTBNyUM47Zg),
  Micah described memories as becoming extracted from synaptic storage to whole-brain activity.
- In the [July 30, 2022 longer SAN explanation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IKbl0ryKRoY),
  he described a theory of memories being stored in synapses and "magnified to the whole brain,"
  with inhibitory waves proposed as part of the mechanism.
- In the [July 31, 2022 NAPOT explanation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fLp-yTQ6pSM),
  he framed the explicit scaling problem, linked it to fractals and inhibition, and proposed that
  an oscillating learned group can restore a memory when any available member receives a cue.
- The [September 18, 2022 fifth-revision explanation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vixhppNAKPs)
  expanded the hierarchy from synapses and dendritic clusters through neurons, arrays, columns,
  pathways, and whole-brain mental models.

These sources establish the SAN problem and proposed architecture. They do not prove that the
architecture is biologically sufficient, that the same trace exists at every scale, or that
oscillatory phase is the only coordinating variable.
