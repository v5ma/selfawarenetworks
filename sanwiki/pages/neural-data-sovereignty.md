**Neural data sovereignty** refers to the question of who owns and controls data decoded from a person's brain — thoughts, visual experiences, and auditory experiences — as [[brain-computer-interface]] (BCI) technology reaches the resolution needed to reconstruct them.

## The Decoding Horizon

Once a BCI can decode the visual cortex, [[auditory-cortex|auditory cortex]], or somatosensory cortex at sufficient resolution, the brain becomes functionally equivalent to "an open book — a hard drive we can just access." This capability is the threshold at which neural data sovereignty becomes a live legal and ethical problem.

The SAN framing is that this capability is not speculative: any sufficiently skilled neuroscientist can already extract information from targeted cortical regions with existing tools; [[neuralink]] and successor devices lower the barrier and scale the access.

## Pain as the Unstoppable Driver

[[gh-resnote04|Micah]]'s argument (2021 panel): BCI adoption cannot be stopped by regulation because it is pulled forward by the economic force of human pain. Medical demand for better treatment of mental injuries, neurological conditions, and chronic pain creates a funding engine that outpaces any legal system. This applies globally — not just to Neuralink but to parallel BCI efforts in multiple countries.

Implication: neural data sovereignty questions must be answered *before* mass deployment, not after, because after deployment the technology will be everywhere and regulation will be reactive.

## Key Ethical Risks

1. **Thought decoding at scale** — governments, corporations, or bad actors accessing neural streams
2. **Pleasure-loop capture** — stimulating reward circuits reduces agency (cf. rat lever-pressing electrode experiments)
3. **Simulation stacking** — connecting BCIs to VR may create addictive virtual realities indistinguishable from external reality
4. **Geopolitical asymmetry** — BCI-enhanced actors vs. unenhanced actors; parallels to nuclear deterrence

## Relation to SAN Theory

The SAN framework's model of consciousness as [[neural-oscillatory-tomography]] — where thoughts are oscillatory patterns in neural arrays — makes the decoding problem tractable in principle. The same model that explains consciousness also explains why neural data can be reconstructed from electrode signals.
