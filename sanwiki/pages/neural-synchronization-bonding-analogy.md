SAN theory uses chemical bonding as a structural analogy for neural oscillatory synchronization. Just as atoms form bonds by sharing or exchanging electrons to achieve lower-energy configurations, neurons and neural arrays form functional "bonds" by synchronizing oscillatory phase to achieve stable collective representations.

The analogy is more than metaphorical — it maps specific bonding regimes to neural coupling types:

| Bond type | Chemical property | Neural equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| Ionic | Strong, rigid transfer | Forced phase-lock; dominant driver entrains followers |
| Covalent | Shared, flexible | Mutual phase adjustment; shared representational state |
| Metallic / Van der Waals | Weak, fluid | Loose gamma coupling; background contextual binding |

## The Entification Corollary

The bonding analogy grounds the [[entification]] principle: cells act as a single unit because they replicate the same function, and that function *magnifies with the area of homogeneous collaborators*. This is analogous to how metallic bonding strengthens as more atoms contribute free electrons — the collective property scales with the count of synchronized participants.

Brainwaves function as attractors in this model: large-scale oscillatory rhythms lower the "activation energy" for local neurons to synchronize, binding distributed representations into a coherent entified whole. See [[brainwave-vortex-attractor]].

## Firefly Synchronization as Model

The biophysical basis of the analogy is oscillator coupling as described by Steven Strogatz (*Sync*): isolated oscillators with slightly different natural frequencies spontaneously synchronize when weakly coupled. Neural populations exploit this same mathematics — [[napot]] arrays couple through shared dendritic fields and gap junctions, spontaneously entifying into collective observers.

## Weak Forces and Neural Plasticity

Hydrogen bonds and Van der Waals forces — weak but reversible — map to the flexible, short-timescale binding that allows conscious contents to shift rapidly. Strong covalent-like bonds correspond to consolidated long-term memories: stable but not easily broken. This two-tier bonding architecture (strong structural + weak dynamic) mirrors the distinction between [[multi-scale-memory]] layers.
