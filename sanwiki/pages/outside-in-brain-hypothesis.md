The **outside-in brain hypothesis** is [[gh-resnote04|Micah]] Blumberg]]'s proposed framework for how the brain processes information — structured around the claim that cortical computation is organized in an outside-in direction: from external sensory inputs inward through progressively more abstract representational layers, rather than driven primarily by top-down prediction or inside-out generative modeling.

## Core Claim

Conventional predictive processing accounts (Friston, Clark) emphasize the brain as an inside-out prediction machine: high-level priors generate top-down predictions; sensory data arrives only to update those predictions. The outside-in hypothesis inverts the emphasis — or more precisely, foregrounds the stimulus-driven, bottom-up formation of oscillatory patterns as the constitutive act of perception, with top-down influence serving to modulate, not dominate.

In SAN terms, [[napot]] describes how neural arrays project oscillatory tomographic representations. The outside-in hypothesis situates the *origin* of these projections in external stimulus structure, not internal prior. Perception is not hallucination corrected by sensation; it is sensation structured by learned coincidence detectors (see [[cellular-oscillating-tomography]]).

## Status and Scope

As of April 2021 ([[gh-a0087z|a0087z]].md), [[gh-resnote04|Micah]] notes: "Even if specific mechanical details prove incorrect, the author suggests the general framework could remain valuable to neuroscience researchers." The hypothesis is offered as a productive organizing framework, not a falsified claim about specific receptor types.

The outside-in framing informs how the SAN theory positions learning: patterns are built from repeated external exposure (experience-dependent coincidence detection), then internalized as phase-tuned templates. The inside — the learned template — always originates from outside.
