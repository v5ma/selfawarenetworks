# Fourier Slice Theorem

**Fourier Slice Theorem** is the mathematical result — that the 1D Fourier transform of the projection of a 2D function along a given direction equals a slice through the 2D Fourier transform of that function along the same direction — that the [[self-aware-network|Self-Aware Networks]] / [[napot-theory|NAPOT]] corpus reaches for whenever it needs to ground its *tomographic* reading of [[cortex]]. In SAN, the [[cortex]] is doing something formally analogous to medical CT reconstruction: gathering [[phase-wave-differentials|phase-wave-differential]] "slices" from columns at different angular positions and reconstructing a coherent volumetric scene from those slices, with the Fourier slice theorem as the mathematical guarantee that the reconstruction is well-posed.

## Post-SAN

The mainstream literature on the Fourier slice theorem is settled (Bracewell, Kak & Slaney's textbook on CT reconstruction). What is SAN-specific is the cross-application of the theorem from CT imaging to *cortical signal processing*. The closest external converger is the cortical-decoding literature that uses CT-style reconstruction methods on multi-electrode recordings; the SAN-side prediction is that *phase-aligned narrow-band sampling* across columns should reconstruct stimulus structure through the same Fourier-slice machinery used in medical imaging — and that the [[dominant-phase-wave-differential-rhythm|dominant rhythm]] in [[cortex]] should be visible as the Fourier-domain "slice" that survives reconstruction. Specific testable consequences: (i) decoding of stimulus structure from multi-column phase samples should improve when the samples are aligned by the angular geometry the Fourier slice theorem requires; (ii) cortical reconstruction of complex scenes should fail in the same way CT reconstruction fails when slices are missing — predictable systematic distortions rather than uniform degradation; (iii) the [[continuous-neural-rendering|continuous-rendering]] frame should show signatures consistent with iterative Fourier-domain reconstruction rather than direct stored-3D-model recall.
