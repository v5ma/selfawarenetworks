The brain uses distinct neural mechanisms when storing a memory versus when retrieving it. The **SPEAR** framework (Separate Phases at Encoding and Retrieval) formalizes this: hippocampal circuits switch modes depending on whether the task is to bind a new pattern or reinstate a stored one.

A 2021 neuroimaging study found that neurons in the human medial temporal lobe track multiple temporal contexts simultaneously during episodic memory processing, suggesting that "time" is an explicit representational dimension, not a passive timestamp.

## Relation to SAN oscillatory model

SAN theory frames the hippocampus as a phase-switching oscillator: during encoding, [[theta-oscillations]] predominate and new [[phase-wave-differentials]] are impressed on CA3-CA1 synapses; during retrieval, the circuit "replays" stored phase patterns and projects them to neocortical arrays. SPEAR is thus a temporal instance of the more general [[napot]] principle: arrays alternate between write-mode (encoding) and read-mode (retrieval) by shifting oscillatory phase.

The finding that stem cells sense neighborhood density via mechanical waves (also referenced in this source) parallels this: cellular state-transitions depend on oscillatory environmental signals, not only on chemical gradients — consistent with SAN's claim that information in biological systems is fundamentally phase-encoded.
