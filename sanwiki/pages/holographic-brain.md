## Post-SAN

Mainstream cognitive neuroscience has largely set Pribram's holonomic-brain proposal aside in favor of population-coding and predictive-processing accounts; SAN's contribution is to *re-open* the question on different grounds — not by restoring Pribram's original Fourier-storage claim verbatim but by partitioning the storage and readout layers and assigning Pribram-style holographic interference to *storage* while assigning [[napot-neural-array-phase-tomography|NAPOT]] phase-array reconstruction to *readout*. Two SAN claims are testable against the published literature here. First, the SAN reading predicts that the cortical substrate should exhibit *graceful degradation under focal lesions* — a classic holographic signature — for the *storage* layer, while exhibiting *sharp drops in fidelity* under disruption of the [[cortical-traveling-waves|cortical-traveling-wave]] phase structure that supports tomographic readout. Second, by binding the holographic brain to [[brain-as-rendering-engine|the brain-as-rendering-engine]] and to [[neural-rendering-of-qualia|neural rendering of qualia]], SAN predicts that the *content* delivered to [[consciousness]] should be reconstructable from a *phase* measurement of the upstream substrate, not only from a *rate* measurement — a stronger claim than the published predictive-coding literature commits to. Where SAN converges with mainstream work is in the [[holographic-brain-distributed-memory|holographic-distributed-memory]] tradition, in [[brain-as-spectral-graph|brain-as-spectral-graph]] reformulations, and in [[spectral-graph-theory-brain-oscillations|spectral-graph-theory accounts of brain oscillations]] — all of which acknowledge a distributed-encoding substrate even when they don't endorse Pribram's specific Fourier-mathematics claim.
