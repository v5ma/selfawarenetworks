The **visual cortex** comprises multiple occipital and adjoining cortical areas that transform and recurrently update visual information. V1 is an early cortical stage, not prefrontal cortex, not the whole visual system, and not a complete internal picture viewed by another agent.

## Established visual pathways

For the canonical geniculostriate route, retinal ganglion-cell axons project to the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN), and LGN projections reach V1 primarily in layer 4, with pathway- and cell-type-specific terminations. Other visual routes involve structures such as the superior colliculus and pulvinar. The retina does not generally project directly to V1 layer 1.

V1 is retinotopically organized and transforms local visual signals. Its activity is shaped by:

- feedforward input from the LGN and earlier visual areas;
- lateral interactions within visual cortex;
- feedback from extrastriate, parietal, temporal, and attention-related systems;
- eye movements, behavioral state, memory, and task demands.

Feedback reaches layer-specific circuits and can carry contextual information. This makes V1 recurrent and context-sensitive without making it the sole site of a visual render.

## Distributed visual construction

Stable visual behavior depends on interactions across V1, extrastriate ventral and dorsal streams, parietal and temporal cortex, thalamic systems, attention and memory networks, oculomotor circuits, and body-related feedback. Object identity, spatial relation, action relevance, and visual stability are therefore distributed achievements.

Traveling waves and phase-sensitive responses are real measurable phenomena in particular preparations. They may organize timing or propagation, but the presence of a wave does not show that the wave *is* the percept. Likewise, a dendritic event can influence a cell's output without containing a whole visual object.

## The SAN/NAPOT interpretation

SAN proposes that visual perception is an active, recurrent, body-linked working model rather than a passive retinal recording. NAPOT interprets repeated many-to-one-to-many transformations across neural populations as a candidate reconstruction process. The strongest version of this proposal is distributed:

1. receptors and early pathways provide partial projections;
2. recurrent cortical and thalamic circuits transform and compare them;
3. memory, attention, eye movement, and body state alter the working model;
4. action samples the world again;
5. new sensory evidence updates the next cycle.

V1 contributes to this loop but does not contain the complete render. There is no little viewer. In SAN terms, the observer is the network-wide tonic state registering phasic changes and altering later cellular, field, and motor activity.

## Tonic canvas and phasic ink

The tonic canvas is the current state of a receiving population, including its excitability, recurrent context, and recent history. Phasic ink is a consequential update relative to that receiver and timescale. These roles are not fixed to alpha, beta, or gamma, and they should not be mapped one-to-one onto cortical layers.

## Decoding is not direct observation

fMRI and BCI reconstructions use an encoding model, measurement transform, statistical decoder, and often an explicit image prior. A recognizable reconstruction shows that measured activity contains recoverable information under that model. It does not show that a literal movie is stored in V1, that the decoder reads visual thought directly, or that the reconstructed pixels are the brain's own representational format.

## Testable SAN questions

A useful NAPOT visual experiment should identify the areas, layers, cell types, direction of influence, phase variable, and perceptual or behavioral outcome. It should ask whether the proposed phase relation or traveling wave explains and causally changes visual updating beyond firing rate, broadband power, stimulus structure, eye movements, attention, common input, and ordinary recurrent connectivity.

Claims about conscious stability need stronger evidence than successful classification. They require selective changes in perception or behavior while basic sensory drive and task engagement remain intact.

- [Zipser, Lamme, and Schiller (1996), contextual modulation in macaque V1](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.16-22-07376.1996)
- [Lee, Blake, and Heeger (2005), traveling waves in V1 during binocular rivalry](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1365)
- [Muckli et al. (2015), contextual feedback to superficial layers of human V1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2015.08.057)
- [Bastos et al. (2015), frequency-dependent feedforward and feedback influence in primate visual cortex](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2014.12.018)
- [Townsend et al. (2022), feedforward and feedback traveling waves in awake visual cortex](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-32378-x)
- [Nishimoto et al. (2011), model-based reconstruction of viewed natural movies from fMRI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2011.08.031)

These findings support recurrent, contextual, wave-like, and decodable visual activity. They do not establish literal cortical tomography, a complete visual screen in V1, or SAN/NAPOT's phenomenal interpretation.
