# Olfactory Neural Pathway

Olfaction reaches primary olfactory cortex without the obligatory first-order thalamic relay used by vision, hearing, and somatosensation. That does **not** mean the thalamus is absent from smell. Olfactory processing branches through bulb, primary olfactory, limbic, thalamic, and orbitofrontal routes rather than moving through one serial chain.

## Receptor to olfactory bulb

Olfactory sensory neurons in the nasal epithelium express members of a large receptor family ([Buck and Axel, 1991](https://doi.org/10.1016/0092-8674%2891%2990418-X)). Axons from neurons expressing a given receptor converge onto a small number of organized glomerular targets in the mouse olfactory bulb ([Mombaerts et al., 1996](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0092-8674%2800%2981387-2)). Mitral and tufted cells then distribute bulb output to several olfactory forebrain targets.

This receptor-linked geometry is not a one-receptor-one-odor or one-glomerulus-one-object code. Odor identity depends on combinations, concentration, timing, state, learning, and downstream population responses.

## Primary olfactory cortex is not prefrontal cortex

The piriform cortex is a principal component of primary olfactory cortex in the ventral forebrain. It is not simply the underside of prefrontal cortex. Odor responses in piriform cortex can form dispersed ensembles without the smooth chemotopic layout seen in the olfactory bulb ([Stettler and Axel, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2009.09.005)).

Bulb output also reaches other primary olfactory and limbic targets. These routes are parallel and recurrent, so the pathway should not be drawn as one mandatory bulb-to-piriform-to-thalamus-to-orbitofrontal sequence.

## Thalamic and orbitofrontal participation

The mediodorsal thalamus participates in later olfactory processing and routes involving orbitofrontal cortex. Human functional-connectivity work found that attention to odor strengthened coupling in an indirect piriform-mediodorsal-thalamus-orbitofrontal route ([Plailly et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.5607-07.2008)). Patients with focal thalamic lesions could retain odor detection while showing impairments in identification and hedonic judgments ([Tham et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2114-09.2009)).

The careful conclusion is therefore:

1. olfactory information can reach primary olfactory cortex without a prior thalamic relay;
2. direct cortical and indirect transthalamic routes both participate in later processing;
3. the role of each route depends on task, attention, identification, valuation, and behavioral state.

## Smell is active sampling

Sniffing is part of the measurement, not background noise. Human imaging separated activity associated with sniffing from activity associated with odorant content while showing that both contribute to olfactory exploration ([Sobel et al., 1998](https://doi.org/10.1038/32654)). In mice, changing when olfactory neurons were activated within the sniff cycle changed discrimination ([Smear et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10521)).

These findings support a sensorimotor loop: breathing changes the chemical sample, receptor and circuit activity changes the current estimate, and the estimate changes later sampling and action.

## SAN interpretation

For SAN, olfaction is a useful test because it defeats any theory that assumes every sense is organized like a camera. Receptor-linked convergence in the bulb, distributed piriform ensembles, active sniff timing, and direct plus transthalamic routes provide different receiver contexts at different scales.

SAN proposes that these partial, state-dependent measurements can participate in a recurrent observer-action architecture. A maintained tonic context and phasic updates may help coordinate odor identity, body state, memory, value, and action across connected populations. The component studies above support the anatomical and physiological pieces; they do not establish a universal phase code, NAPOT reconstruction, one meaning-attribution nucleus, or consciousness.

No neuron, glomerulus, cortical area, or thalamic nucleus is the hidden viewer. The distributed network changes its own state and behavior in response to the measurements.
