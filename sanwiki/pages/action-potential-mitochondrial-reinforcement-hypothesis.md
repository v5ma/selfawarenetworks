# Action-Potential/Mitochondrial Reinforcement Hypothesis

## The idea in plain language

Neural activity costs energy. Mitochondria supply ATP, and active synapses need that ATP for
transmission, protein production, and plasticity. This makes local energy supply a plausible part of
how repeatedly used pathways are maintained or strengthened.

SAN proposed a more specific mechanism: the electromagnetic change produced by an action potential
might affect flavin-containing mitochondrial processes and increase ATP production, creating a
local reinforcement signal.

## What is known

Experiments support these narrower facts:

- activity raises local energy demand;
- calcium and energy-sensing pathways change mitochondrial behavior;
- mitochondria can move to or remain near active synapses;
- local mitochondrial ATP supports synaptic function and plasticity.

## What remains a SAN hypothesis

There is not yet direct evidence that an action potential's own electromagnetic field activates
mitochondrial flavins and serves as a reward signal. That idea remains valuable because it can be
separated from the known calcium and metabolic pathways and tested.

## Why the distinction matters

Rejecting the untested magnetic-flavin link would not erase the broader insight that local energy
availability can bias learning. Confirming it would identify an additional activity-to-plasticity
route.

## Energy constrains action without becoming willpower

The owner-dated October 26, 2013 material in [[gh-a0503z|a0503z]] belongs to the same conversation
family as `a0151z`; it is not independent confirmation. Its legitimate operation is that metabolic
and bodily condition constrain which actions a nervous system can prepare, sustain, and learn
from. Sleep, health, arousal, neuromodulation, learned value, expected consequence, and available
metabolic support all contribute.

ATP is an implementation cost of neural and muscular work, not a scalar unit of willpower,
motivation, or depression. Historical diet and supplement suggestions remain source history rather
than current medical guidance.
