# Conscious Episodes and the Continuity of a Person

## The problem

The nervous system never holds perfectly still. Brain activity changes from moment to moment.
Sleep, learning, injury, development, and new experience alter it further. Yet a person can retain
memories, skills, commitments, body ownership, relationships, and recognizable ways of responding.

A theory of the self therefore has to explain two things at once:

1. why present experience changes; and
2. how one person can remain continuous across those changes.

## The operation in plain language

Long-lived neural and bodily organization constrains what can happen now. Current sensory input,
memory, and body state then recruit and modify a present self-and-world state. That state helps
select an action. The action changes the body or environment, and its consequences return as new
evidence. Learning preserves part of the result for later.

```text
durable learned neural and bodily organization
-> present recurrent self/world state
-> sensory and remembered differences
-> prediction and action
-> bodily and environmental consequence
-> returned evidence and plastic change
-> later recurrent episode
```

No little observer watches this process. The distributed system itself receives, compares,
remembers, updates, and acts.

After that operation is clear, two terms help:

- A **conscious episode** is the currently enacted period of experience and access.
- **Diachronic self-continuity** is the relation that connects one person across different
  episodes and physical changes over time.

Continuity does not require exact repetition of every molecule, spike, or field value.

## Established footholds

Dreaming can occur during both REM and NREM sleep. Posterior cortical activity also differs between
reported dream experience and no reported experience
([Siclari et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.4545)).
TMS-EEG work shows that effective cortical response propagation can change substantially during
NREM sleep compared with wakefulness
([Massimini et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1117256)).
These findings support treating sleep as a change in conscious organization, not destruction of
the person.

In mice, optogenetic reactivation of a tagged hippocampal ensemble can reinstate a learned
context-specific fear response
([Liu et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature11028)).
Preparation-specific work also shows how persistent molecular function involved in long-term
potentiation can survive protein turnover
([Lee et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2211572119)).
These are bounded examples of later activity being constrained by learned organization.

Injury can change personality and social behavior. Ventromedial prefrontal lesion research
provides one clinical foothold
([Barrash et al., 2000](https://doi.org/10.1207/S1532694205Barrash)).
Split-brain findings additionally stress-test simple accounts of unified access and report, while
remaining contested as a direct count of conscious subjects
([Pinto et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/aww358)).

Neural currents generate extracellular fields, and weak fields can influence timing in bounded
preparations
([Frohlich and McCormick, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2010.06.005);
[Reato et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2059-10.2010)).
This makes field-cell interaction a legitimate physical variable.

## Where the comparison stops

- Ordinary sleep does not erase the person, and dreaming is not restricted to one sleep stage.
- Reactivating one ensemble does not make that ensemble the whole self.
- Memory, molecular persistence, embodiment, or one connectivity pattern is not sufficient by
  itself for personhood.
- Injury can alter a person without every neural change creating a new person.
- Plasticity can support recovery without guaranteeing complete restoration.
- Split-brain findings constrain access and integration but do not supply a universal one-person
  or two-person verdict.
- Local field effects do not establish that a global electromagnetic field is the whole self.
- One instantaneous field map or spike pattern is not the longer-lived person.
- Neural recurrence does not validate a cosmological or spacetime theory.

## The SAN extension

SAN proposes that continuity across episodes should be measured as a bundle:

1. memory continuity;
2. embodied and interoceptive continuity;
3. perspective continuity;
4. causal recurrence through perception, action, and returned consequence;
5. plastic persistence of learned constraints; and
6. policy continuity in values, goals, and characteristic choices.

The hypothesis is that a personal self is a changing constraint on its own future. Earlier states
shape what can be perceived, remembered, and chosen now. Present action changes the next body and
world state, and learning alters what later episodes can become.

The Field-Cell Self is a stronger extension of this account. Cellular, synaptic, chemical,
axonal, gap-junction, bodily, and extracellular-field interactions jointly contribute to the
current episode. Field-cell variables count as a distinctive mechanism only if they explain or
predict content, integration, or continuity beyond matched models using spikes, synapses, anatomy,
arousal, and common input alone.

## Discriminating tests

- compare memory, embodiment, perspective, action attribution, and characteristic policy before,
  during, and after sleep or anesthesia;
- distinguish temporary loss of access from loss of stored organization;
- measure continuity and divergence after lesion, stimulation, amnesia, or recovery;
- compare one-state identity models with multi-variable continuity models;
- perturb local field geometry while controlling for spikes, common input, and arousal;
- test whether later episodes are better predicted by durable organization plus current context
  than by either alone; and
- apply the same continuity bundle to temporary human-machine coupling and artificial systems.

The SAN hypothesis must be revised if the bundle adds no predictive or explanatory value beyond
simpler memory, behavior, connectivity, or social-identity models.
