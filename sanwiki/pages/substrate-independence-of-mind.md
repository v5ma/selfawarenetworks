*Substrate independence of mind* is the hypothesis that consciousness and intelligence
can arise in any physical medium that instantiates the relevant computational and
adaptive structure — not only in biological neurons. SAN engages this question
critically: the patterns matter, but the substrate may impose non-trivial constraints
on which patterns can exist.

## The core claim (substrate-independence view)

A mind is constituted by its information-processing patterns, not by the specific atoms
or molecules implementing those patterns. A fiber-optic network, a photonic computer,
or a system using protons instead of electrons could in principle support consciousness
if it reproduces the relevant [[distributed-phase-representations]] and [[gh-a0206z|self]]-modification
dynamics.

This view is consistent with SAN's emphasis on information patterns: [[napot]] describes
the oscillatory pattern as primary; the neuron is just the carrier. [[infogenetics]]
pushes this further — if information patterns alone can trigger neural responses, then
the pattern truly dominates the substrate.

## The constraint argument (partial substrate-dependence)

Critics in [[gh-a0409z|a0409z]].md argue that synapses are not transferable to light-based systems
without fundamental redesign: the synaptic plasticity mechanism (voltage-gated ion
channels, neurotransmitter release, dendritic-spine enlargement) is not substrate-neutral.
[[gh-a0206z|Self]]-modification — the ability to alter connection weights through activity — may
require specific electrochemical dynamics. Without [[gh-a0206z|self]]-modification, a system can
compute but cannot learn; whether it can be conscious without learning is an open question.

## SAN's nuanced position

SAN treats the oscillatory pattern as the carrier of consciousness, not the neuron —
this leans substrate-independent. But [[neural-array-projection]] shows that the
physical dendritic mechanics matter for *how* projection is shaped. SAN therefore
supports substrate independence for the *representational* layer but acknowledges
substrate constraints at the *plasticity* layer.
