# Dendritic Pattern Detection

**Dendritic pattern detection** describes the ability of dendritic compartments and whole neurons to respond differently to the location, timing, order, and combination of synaptic inputs. Active conductances, branch geometry, excitation, inhibition, and plasticity can make the response nonlinear. This supports selective computation, but it does not make every neuron a universal semantic classifier.

> **Scientific boundary:** The detected pattern is defined by the preparation, inputs, cell type, branch, time window, and measured output. Single-cell selectivity, local-circuit representation, and distributed semantic recognition are different scales and must not be collapsed into one claim.

## What single dendrites can discriminate

Thin dendritic branches can act as nonlinear integration compartments. Nearby inputs on one branch can sum differently from inputs distributed across branches. In cortical pyramidal cells, experimentally delivered temporal sequences produced different local calcium signals, somatic depolarization, and spike output depending on input order and direction. See [Polsky et al. (2004)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1253) and [Branco et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1189664).

In vivo, local dendritic spikes have also been associated with enhanced orientation selectivity in mouse visual cortex. See [Smith et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature12600). These results establish specific forms of dendritic selectivity. They do not establish arbitrary concept classification by one cell.

A weighted threshold model can already distinguish some input patterns because synaptic weights, locations, signs, and timing differ. Active dendrites add branch-local nonlinearities, regenerative events, and state dependence; they do not replace threshold integration with an entirely separate principle.

## Excitation and inhibition are not a simple jury vote

The "jury" metaphor is useful if bounded. Excitatory and inhibitory conductances influence membrane voltage and dendritic electrogenesis, but inhibition is not always a scalar vote against firing. Its effect depends on reversal potential, timing, location, receptor type, chloride regulation, and network disinhibition. Dendrite-targeting inhibition can gate nonlinear dendritic events and burst output in circuit-specific ways. See [Lovett-Barron et al. (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.3024).

There is no universal 5 ms integration window. Relevant windows range from submillisecond coincidence to tens or hundreds of milliseconds depending on receptor kinetics, membrane state, dendritic events, oscillatory context, and plasticity rule.

## Plasticity changes future detection criteria

NMDA receptors are voltage- and ligand-dependent channels. Coincident glutamate binding and depolarization can relieve magnesium block, permit current, and contribute to calcium-dependent plasticity. A receptor is not simply retimed after one detection event to admit a new pattern class.

Long-term potentiation and depression can alter receptor number, conductance, spine structure, release probability, and network recruitment over multiple timescales. Protein synthesis is required for some longer-lasting forms, not every immediate change in selectivity. [[Criterial-Causation|Criteria causation]] can remain as a conceptual interpretation of how present activity changes future response conditions, but it must not be substituted for a measured molecular sequence.

## Apical and basal compartments

Pyramidal neurons receive heterogeneous excitatory, inhibitory, thalamic, cortical, and modulatory inputs across apical, basal, oblique, somatic, and axonal compartments. Some sensory-cortex pathways preferentially place higher-order or feedback input in superficial layers and feedforward input nearer basal or perisomatic regions, but apical does not universally mean top-down and basal does not universally mean bottom-up.

For example, mouse V1 layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons receive heterogeneous inputs in both domains, and orientation tuning was robust to removal of the apical tuft in one preparation. See [Park et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-13029-0). Layer 5 apical calcium events can influence burst firing and perceptual behavior in specific circuits, but that does not define one canonical conscious-access gate for the entire cortex. See [Takahashi et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-020-0677-8).

## Scale ladder

- **Micro:** a branch or neuron discriminates defined spatiotemporal input configurations.
- **Meso:** a local circuit recruits cell populations through recurrent excitation, inhibition, and plasticity.
- **Macro:** distributed systems support invariant recognition, context, memory, body state, and action.

A highly selective "concept cell" does not contain a concept by itself. Its response is embedded in an ensemble, depends on task and recording conditions, and can generalize across several representations associated with a person or concept. Exact neuron counts such as one cell, 80-100 cells, or 20 million cells are not universal boundaries between these scales.

## Bounded SAN interpretation

SAN proposes that nested cellular and population selectivity contributes to distributed observer-action. The network, not a hidden viewer or one classifier neuron, registers changes through its ongoing cellular, synaptic, field, body, and action state.

A discriminating SAN experiment should define the candidate pattern, receiver, scale, and output, then compare dendritic features against matched models using total excitation, inhibition, spike timing, firing rate, connectivity, common input, history, and behavioral state. A finding at one scale cannot be promoted to NAPOT or consciousness without an explicit bridge and new evidence.
