fNIRS (Functional Near Infrared Spectroscopy) is a non-invasive neuroimaging technique that measures blood oxygenation changes in the brain using near-infrared light passed through the skull — a "poor man's fMRI" capable of real-time biofeedback.

## How it works

fNIRS shines near-infrared light through the skull and detects differential absorption by oxygenated vs. deoxygenated hemoglobin. Unlike [[fmri]], it is portable, tolerant of movement, and can run at consumer-accessible cost. Primary applications: [[neurofeedback]] (biofeedback training) and pure hemodynamic measurement.

## Lineage: HEG

fNIRS descends from HEG ([[hemo-encephalography]]), patented in 1999 by Hershel Toomim and Robert Marsh. HEG uses light to observe cerebral blood flow through the skull. Jonathan Toomim (Hershel's grandson) extended HEG research by removing the low-frequency filter that had prevented earlier instruments from detecting ultra-slow cerebrovascular oscillations.

## Connection to SAN

fNIRS is one of the sensing modalities that motivates the [[self-aware-networks]] theory: if external light can read hemodynamic correlates of neural firing, then the brain's own internal oscillatory signals may similarly "read" distributed network activity — a key intuition behind [[tomographic-phase-wave]] and [[neural-array-projection-oscillation-tomography]].

## Community context

Jonathan Toomim presented fNIRS at NeurotechX San Francisco (Red Victorian, Feb 2019). The NeurotechX group simultaneously explored connecting EEG to WebXR via A-Frame/Three.js — an early precursor to the v5ma spatial computing stack. The group also investigated deep learning on 3D point clouds (lidar, fMRI, fNIRS volumes) for semantic segmentation, bridging neuroimaging with spatial computing.
