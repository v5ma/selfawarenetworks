# Brain Plasticity

> **Image status:** Both earlier figures failed medical review and are quarantined. They remain diagnostic records, not publication art. Their recoverable core was a scale transition: repeated, state-gated activity can alter eligible cellular and synaptic processes, which can later change circuit recruitment and behavior. The figures incorrectly made injury, neurofeedback, and entrainment look like direct inputs to one universal rewiring pathway; called a generic timing cartoon a "Hebbian timing window"; and presented network cooperation as a simple anatomical end state. Replacement art remains paused.

**Brain plasticity** is the capacity of nervous systems to change with activity, experience, development, injury, and training. The term covers several mechanisms and timescales rather than one generic rewiring process. These include short- and long-term changes in synaptic efficacy, structural changes in dendritic spines and axons, shifts in inhibition and excitability, altered sensory and motor maps, and learned changes in how distributed populations use available signals.

## What is established

Spike timing can change synaptic efficacy under defined preparations. In cultured hippocampal neurons, the relative timing of pre- and postsynaptic activity determined the direction and magnitude of synaptic modification ([Bi and Poo, 1998](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.18-24-10464.1998)). This is one important plasticity rule, not a universal rule for every synapse, region, state, or timescale.

Structural change can outlast a training episode. Longitudinal imaging in mouse cortex showed learning- and experience-related dendritic-spine formation and elimination, with a subset of new and developmentally retained spines persisting for long periods ([Yang, Pan, and Gan, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08577)). A persistent spine is not automatically one stored memory, and memory is not confined to spine count.

Adult cortical function can also be modulated under bounded experimental conditions. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation transiently altered contrast sensitivity in adults with amblyopia in the tested protocol ([Thompson et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2008.06.052)). This result does not establish that generic stimulation increases intelligence, permanently rewires cortex, or validates a commercial entrainment protocol.

Oscillatory phase can matter to plasticity in particular preparations. In rat visual-cortex slices, synchronized 20 or 40 Hz input produced LTP when excitatory postsynaptic potentials arrived near membrane-potential peaks and LTD when they arrived near troughs ([Wespatat, Tennigkeit, and Singer, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2221-04.2004)). In hippocampal CA1 slices, a burst delivered at different phases of a cholinergically induced theta oscillation could produce bidirectional synaptic plasticity ([Huerta and Lisman, 1995](https://doi.org/10.1016%2F0896-6273%2895%2990094-2)). These experiments provide a biological bridge between oscillatory timing and LTP/LTD. They do not establish one universal phase rule, whole-brain phase locking, or SAN's stronger rendering account.

## Rapid reallocation is not anatomical reassignment

A January 2013 SAN source used the example that, after five days of blindfolding, visual cortex could "start working for other parts" of the mind. The nearest primary experiment is more precise. Normally sighted adults underwent five days of complete blindfolding plus intensive tactile training. Tactile stimulation then recruited occipital cortex, and disrupting that cortex impaired Braille discrimination; both effects were absent 24 hours after blindfold removal ([Merabet et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0003046)). The authors interpreted the rapid, reversible effect as likely unmasking of pre-existing connections and shifts in connectivity, not immediate creation of new sensory anatomy.

The source-faithful reading is therefore **dynamic recruitment of available circuitry under changed input and training**, not one sense permanently taking ownership of another cortex. The 2013 source also proposed that limited processing resources and dominant attractor-like patterns help determine what reaches awareness. That resource-allocation-to-conscious-content connection remains a SAN hypothesis rather than a result established by the blindfold study.

Sensory substitution supplies a related but distinct example. Participants learned to identify sound categories from real-time spatiotemporal vibration patterns delivered by a four-motor wristband ([Perrotta, Asgeirsdottir, and Eagleman, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroscience.2021.01.008)). The study supports learned use of a transformed signal through an intact tactile pathway. It does not show that cortical areas are universally interchangeable, that any dataset can become a percept without training, or that tactile cortex literally becomes auditory cortex.

This evidence fits the receiver-relative principle used elsewhere in SAN: a pattern has an effect because a particular receiver, with a particular history and state, has learned how to respond to it. The conservative claim is that training can change the mapping from structured input to discrimination and action. The stronger claim that the mapping is implemented by a SAN-specific phase-wave code remains open.

## Recovered SAN development

The source record contains several successive propositions. They should not be collapsed into one date or one level of evidence.

### 2012: a closed brain-state-to-feedback-to-action loop

Sources dated July and August 2012 describe [[neo-mind-cycle|Neo Mind Cycle]] as more than a display of EEG measurements. Their proposed loop was:

1. measure a changing brain-signal feature;
2. transform that measurement into contingent sound and light;
3. let the participant perceive the changing feedback;
4. let the participant alter attention, imagery, feeling, or another strategy;
5. measure the resulting brain state; and
6. generate the next feedback state.

This is an early **closed-loop learning architecture**. Its strongest recoverable idea is that a receiver can learn relations among internal strategy, measured state, sensory consequence, and subsequent action. It does not by itself prove increased intelligence, generalized clinical efficacy, neurogenesis, or beneficial synaptic rewiring. The dated sources were publicly Git-fixed in June 2022: [[gh-a0017z|a0017z]], [[gh-a0058z|a0058z]], and [[gh-a0284z|a0284z]].

The source emphasis on an interesting and engaging experience is medically interpretable as a proposal about attention, reward, arousal, motivation, and adherence affecting learning conditions. It is not a universal rule that learning requires conscious interest. State-contingent fMRI feedback improved sustained attention relative to feedback from another participant's brain in one controlled study ([deBettencourt et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.3940)), while task-irrelevant perceptual learning has occurred without directed attention when a subliminal feature was paired with successful task events ([Seitz and Watanabe, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1038/422036a)). Engagement can shape a learning context; it is neither synonymous with plasticity nor always required for it.

### 2013: resource allocation, recruitment, and conscious dominance

The January 2013 sources [[gh-a0093z|a0093z]] and [[gh-a0095z|a0095z]] joined short-term sensory recruitment to a broader proposal: changing input alters which distributed patterns occupy limited processing resources, and relatively dominant, incompletely compressed patterns contribute to conscious contents. The blindfold example has empirical support at the level of rapid reversible recruitment. The proposed bridge from resource allocation to dominant conscious attractor patterns remains a SAN interpretation.

### 2022: coherence and separation as complementary plasticity operators

The June 2022 source [[gh-a0142z|a0142z]] argued that coherence can support binding, convergence, and coupling, while structured phase separation or decoherence can support distinction, deconvergence, and decoupling. It then connected microscopic persistence to dendritic and LTP-related structure and proposed that larger inhibitory or LTD-related configurations could organize longer-lived oscillatory routes.

This should not be reduced to "synchrony is good." The SAN proposition is bidirectional: repeated coactivity and phase alignment may stabilize some receiver relations, while inhibition, relative mistiming, or structured phase separation may weaken or separate others. The Wespatat and Huerta-Lisman experiments show that oscillatory phase can select LTP versus LTD under specific conditions. They do not prove that all coherent pairs potentiate, all splayed pairs depress, or that these changes directly determine qualia.

### 2024: PWD as a receiver-relative update

The later [[phase-wave-differentials|PWD]] formulation in `02san.md`, publicly Git-fixed in September 2024, described unexpected excitation or inhibition as a departure from a tonic oscillatory expectation and proposed that repeated receiver-relative departures can alter tuning and effective routing. This is a later extension of the 2022 coherence/separation proposal, not wording that should be backdated into the 2012 or 2013 sources.

## The integrated SAN interpretation

The SAN corpus treats plasticity as the mechanism by which receptors, dendrites, cells, and circuits acquire criteria for later recognition and action. In that reading:

1. repeated activity exposes relations among inputs, internal state, and consequences;
2. local timing, membrane state, inhibition, neuromodulation, and prior history determine which processes are eligible to change;
3. eligible synapses, dendrites, intrinsic excitability, or circuit relations change;
4. the changed receiver responds differently to a later signal;
5. repeated local changes alter recruitment and effective routing across populations; and
6. changed perception and action feed a new state back into the next learning cycle.

SAN further proposes that tonic population state supplies a continuing receiver context while phasic departures carry differentiated updates. This is not merely decorative language in the theory: it is a proposed operational division. To earn biological support, however, a SAN experiment must show that declared phase, waveform, duration, magnitude, or spatial relations predict plastic change beyond spike count, power, arousal, movement, reward, and established timing rules.

SAN also interprets competition and cooperation as descriptions at different scales. Inputs, synapses, assemblies, and actions can compete for influence, timing windows, inhibition, or biological resources while the resulting organism coordinates a single behavior. Cross-modal recruitment can therefore involve local competition and system-level cooperation at the same time. Plasticity evidence does not make either metaphor universally sufficient.

## Neurofeedback and entrainment boundary

The early Neo Mind Cycle notes explicitly connected neurofeedback, audiovisual stimulation, engagement, and brain plasticity. That history is part of SAN's development. It is not clinical validation.

One EEG-neurofeedback experiment found a short-lived increase in human corticospinal excitability after participants learned to reduce alpha-band activity, consistent with a bounded change in motor-cortex excitability ([Ros et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-9568.2010.07100.x)). Neurofeedback can support learned regulation in some task- and protocol-specific settings, but mechanisms, controls, transfer, and clinical effects vary ([Sitaram et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn.2016.164)). A changing EEG feature does not establish beneficial synaptic plasticity, and an isochronic or rhythmic stimulus does not automatically increase plasticity, cognition, confidence, or mental flexibility. Each intervention needs a defined target, control condition, adverse-effect assessment, transfer test, and follow-up.

## What would test the SAN addition

- Compare SAN phase variables with rate-, power-, reward-, and spike-timing-matched models.
- Perturb the proposed timing relation while preserving general arousal and task performance.
- Test whether a learned receiver rule transfers across sessions, contexts, and input carriers.
- Measure both cellular change and the later behavior it is proposed to alter.
- Compare closed-loop, participant-contingent feedback with replayed, yoked, and noncontingent feedback.
- Test whether structured phase separation predicts LTD or route suppression better than ordinary spike timing and inhibition alone.
- Predefine a result that would favor ordinary plasticity mechanisms without the SAN extension.
