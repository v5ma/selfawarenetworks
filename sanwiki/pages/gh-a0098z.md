(oscillat, cortex)

Christof Koch's lecture, WSU: The Biology of Consciousness with Christof Koch, is available on YouTube. At about the 36-minute mark, he begins sharing slides about an experiment that illustrates why consciousness may involve higher-level cortical areas more than lower-level sensory areas.

Watanabe et al. (Science, 2011) ask participants to look at the middle of the screen:
"dissociate [attention](attention.md) & consciousness"
The experiment masks or camouflages a temporal pattern and watches the brain respond to changes in movement, even when the person is not conscious of the change.

The response is modulated by what the person attends to, but the notes do not specify the contrasting condition.

The primary sensory cortex can track things that a person is not conscious of. This fits with the anesthesia study described in these notes, where disrupted apical pyramidal neuron function at the tops of cortical columns may interfere with long-distance integration between brain regions. Under that interpretation, anesthesia disrupts globally coordinated brain activity, so local stimulation alone does not make the person conscious.

The notes compare this point to an fNIRS study on hemispheric lateralization, where attending to the right or left while moving the eyes causes a shift in blood flow.

My argument is that activity in rich-club networks of long-distance interneurons synchronizes cortical columns across different brain regions and may give rise to consciousness. In this framing, consciousness is a form of contextual symmetry: each cortical column acts like a matrix of information, and conscious experience emerges when many such matrices achieve temporal, oscillatory synchronization. The integration is comparable to frames in a movie, with each active interlinked cortical column representing another frame in the movie of consciousness and with multiple frames active simultaneously to present complex spatial patterns.
