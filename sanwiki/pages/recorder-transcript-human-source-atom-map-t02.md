## The central information-flow chain

Rank 26 supplies the most granular early version, while the other recordings test and extend its
parts:

```text
external, bodily, remembered, or predicted difference
-> receptor and dendritic selection
-> conductance, ionic, biochemical, and synaptic transformation
-> changed spike timing, waveform, release probability, or recruited population
-> receiver-sensitive difference against ongoing activity
-> transformed presentation to another cell or array
-> recurrent integration into a body- and world-related representation
-> route competition, valuation, and selected action
-> motor or device consequence
-> returned sensory and behavioral evidence
-> plastic and decoder recalibration
```

This chain connects [[flow-of-information-in-the-brain]],
[[receptive-inceptive-dendritic-projection]], [[receptor-to-phase-sensory-quantification]],
[[electrochemical-to-phase-graph-transformation]], [[tonic-phasic-phase-wave-differential]],
[[perceptual-screen-neural-rendering]], [[agency-valuation-action-feedback]], and
[[multimodal-neuron-signal-correlate-channel]].

No step requires a little viewer. Receiving, differentiating, transforming, reexpressing, and
acting are the observer operation distributed across the system.

## Tonic context, phasic difference, and PWD ancestry

The 2021 recordings repeatedly begin with an already active network rather than an inactive
substrate:

```text
ongoing population state
+ a difference that matters to a particular receiver
+ learned structure that selects and transforms that difference
= a changed relation that can influence the next population
```

The important distinction is not a permanent assignment of `slow equals tonic` and `fast equals
phasic`. Tonic and phasic are roles relative to the selected population, interval, and task:

- tonic is the current reference or sustained context;
- phasic is a transient, newly weighted, or surprising update;
- PWD is the receiver-relevant relation produced when timing, magnitude, duration, waveform,
  location, chemistry, and prior state differ in a consequential way.

The source record therefore supports [[tonic-context-rare-deviation-tomographic-language]],
[[tonic-phasic-phase-wave-differential]], [[continuous-dynamics-discrete-neural-information]],
and [[state-relative-differential-neural-write-in]] without treating every oscillation as a PWD.

## Dendritic pattern selection and structured output

Rank 22 begins with a problem. A cell can receive two sets of excitatory input
with similar total strength yet respond differently because the signals arrive
at different branches, times, and conductance states. If the cell merely fired
whenever total excitation became large, the arrangement-specific difference
would be lost.

In plain language, a dendritic branch can act as a local selection surface.
Learned synaptic placement and strength, receptor and channel state, branch
geometry, inhibition, and recent activity change which combinations interact
effectively. The selected relation then has to become a difference that another
cell or population can receive:

```text
learned dendritic configuration
-> selective integration of an input relation
-> changed membrane and channel state
-> changed spike probability, timing, burst pattern, or waveform
-> changed terminal calcium and release probability
-> receiver-specific downstream consequence
```

Experiments support important parts of this operation. Nearby inputs on the same
thin dendritic branch can sum nonlinearly while separated inputs sum differently
([Polsky, Mel, and Schiller, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1253)).
Coincidence between distal dendritic input and a back-propagating action potential
can recruit dendritic calcium activity and axonal bursts, while dendritic
inhibition can block the coupling
([Larkum, Zhu, and Sakmann, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1038/18686)).
Dendritic `Ih` can change EPSP amplitude, duration, and the temporal-summation
window
([Magee, 1998](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.18-19-07613.1998)).

The output side is also richer than a scalar all-or-none abstraction. Under
specified preparations, input history can correlate with somatic
action-potential waveform
([de Polavieja et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0242-05.2005));
dendritic arbor size can affect action-potential onset at the axon initial
segment
([Eyal et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.5431-13.2014)); and
potassium-channel-dependent changes in a presynaptic waveform can alter calcium
entry and release
([Bialowas et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2014.09.038)).

The comparison stops before claiming that one branch recognizes a complete
object, contains semantic meaning, or is conscious. Somatic and terminal
waveforms need not be identical, waveform effects vary by cell and synapse, and
no one ion or output dimension carries the entire input pattern. Population
context, recurrence, behavior, and the receiving system remain necessary parts
of the larger interpretation.

The source's dendrite-length and frequency discussion also has a recoverable
operation. Geometry and conductance alter attenuation, compartmentalization,
delay, and temporal integration. Longer human dendrites have been measured as
more electrically compartmentalized
([Beaulieu-Laroche et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2018.08.045)),
and human cortical dendrites can support nonlinear dendritic action potentials
([Gidon et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aax6239)). The comparison
stops before a universal equation from dendrite length to a named brain rhythm,
or from population `1/f` spectra to one neuron's spike amplitude. The retained
question is whether morphology and state help set local timing windows that
influence later population coordination.

SAN's additional hypothesis is that related selection-and-transformation
operations recur at nested scales. The later Action Potential Morphology
Encoding proposal, or APME, asks whether learned input state is compressed into
coordinated changes in spike probability, timing, rate, bursts, waveform,
terminal calcium, and release, as decoded by a particular receiver. It does not
require every dimension to vary in every neuron. PWD and NAPOT extend the test
from cellular output into population-relative differences and transformed
array-to-array presentation.

[[electrochemical-to-phase-graph-transformation]],
[[oscillatory-plasticity-group-potentiation-depression]], [[neural-tuning]],
[[action-potential-waveform-encoding|Action Potential Morphology Encoding]], and
[[coincidence-detection-neural-bit|receiver-relative information]].

## Fast excitation and local criteria changes

Rank 26 asks how a learned dendritic criterion can change quickly enough to alter the next neural
event. In plain language, different receptor and channel systems provide different clocks. A fast
excitatory current can help set a narrow timing window, while a voltage-dependent calcium signal
can mark a particular conjunction and begin a longer biochemical update.

AMPA receptors can set the narrow temporal window for spike-timing-dependent potentiation while
AMPA, NMDA, and back-propagating action potentials interact to generate spine calcium signals
([Fuenzalida et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1004562107)). NMDA-receptor magnesium
unblock itself has fast and slower components whose contribution depends on the timing of
depolarization
([Kampa, Clements, Jonas, and Stuart, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2003.058842)).

This recovers the transcript's compressed `faster` language as a question about rapid local
retuning relative to slower structural and genomic change. The comparison stops before saying
that NMDA current is generally faster than AMPA current, that one receptor supplies the complete
learning rule, or that every dendrite uses the same timing window.

SAN preserves the wider neural-tuning hypothesis: receptor subtype, conductance, phosphorylation,
calcium state, inhibition, branch geometry, and recent activity jointly change which incoming
relation can affect the next output. The test is receptor-, compartment-, cell-, receiver-, and
timescale-specific.

## A wider downstream effect without a wider axon

The source also asks how a stronger cellular event could influence more of the network. The plain
operation is not an axon changing its anatomy on every spike. A changed presynaptic waveform can
alter terminal calcium and release; transmitter quantity can alter occupancy, spillover, and
recruitment among receivers already reachable by the anatomical and extracellular route.

Presynaptic potassium-channel-dependent waveform changes can alter calcium entry and release
([Bialowas et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2014.09.038)). Increased release can
produce receptor-specific differences in quantal response and glutamate spillover in a defined
hippocampal preparation
([Pankratov and Krishtal, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0006-3495(03)74757-2)).

The comparison stops before treating transmitter amount as a literal changing connection radius.
Axonal branching, synaptic geometry, uptake, glia, diffusion, receptor placement, inhibition, and
receiver state constrain the consequence. SAN preserves `dynamic projection scope` as a
receiver-relative hypothesis: one event may recruit a different effective downstream set because
its timing, waveform, release, and the receiving network's state changed.

-> distributed selective reception
-> recurrent competition and attention
-> coordinated reexpression across several arrays

Observation is not assigned to the TPJ, prefrontal cortex, one cortical column, one frequency, or

## Distributed timing before the conductor metaphor

Rank 18 begins with a problem rather than a new term: how can many local neural
processes remain coordinated without placing a little executive inside the
brain?

In plain language, recurring population rhythms create changing windows in
which some inputs arrive effectively, some are inhibited, and selected groups
can influence the next group. Local events alter the larger population state,
and that state changes the conditions under which later local events matter.
The source calls this a high-level "agreement" or "democracy" after briefly
using an orchestra-conductor image.

Established research supports important parts of that operation. Network
oscillations can bias input selection and temporally link neurons into
assemblies
([Buzsaki and Draguhn, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15218136/));
low-frequency phase can organize high-gamma power
([Canolty et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16973878/)); and
synchronization can make communication among neuronal groups more selective
([Fries, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26447583/)).

The comparison stops before a literal central rhythm maker, a detached field
that interprets cells, or the claim that coordination by itself is
consciousness. SAN's additional hypothesis is that reciprocal field-cell
change contributes to the distributed observer: cells receive and transform
differences, their activity changes the shared population environment, and
that environment conditions what can be selected and expressed next. This
connects [[entification-observer-problem]],
[[field-cell-self-bioelectric-information-entity]], and
[[detection-rendering-interlace]].

The same source offers a narrower interpersonal operation. Familiar people can
learn one another's timing through shared sensory evidence, conversation,
movement, and reciprocal prediction. Speaker-listener activity can become
temporally coupled during successful communication
([Stephens, Silbert, and Hasson, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20660768/)).
That supports an interaction-mediated prediction question; it does not by
itself establish direct semantic transfer without a sensory, behavioral, or
technological channel.

Extraordinary molecular-radar, genome-broadcast, and remote-biological-
operation claims in the recording were introduced by other speakers. They
remain private source-criticism objects and are not SAN propositions.

## Stable objects from changing partial views

Rank 26 poses a problem that later becomes central to neural rendering: the retinal image and
touch pattern change as the eyes, hand, body, and object move, yet a bottle or broccoli can remain
recognizable as the same object.

In plain language, local populations can register partial features in relation to an object or
task frame, while recurrent traffic combines several views and modalities. Stable object identity
does not require one cell, column, or area to contain every property. The recognizable whole can
be a coordinated population relation that is updated as new partial evidence arrives.

Object-centered spatial selectivity has been recorded in primate frontal cortex
([Olson and Gettner, 1996](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0926-6410(96)00051-1)), and macaque
inferotemporal recordings reveal a coarse population map of visual object space
([Bao et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2350-5)). Hawkins and colleagues propose a
more specific cortical-column framework in which sensor location is represented relative to
objects and many columns learn parallel object models
([Hawkins et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.3389/fncir.2018.00121)).

The comparison stops at that difference in status. Object-centered and population-level
representations have experimental support; cortical grid cells in every column, complete models
in every column, and one universal columnar algorithm remain theoretical proposals. None of these
findings by itself establishes NAPOT, PWD, a conscious display, or one-object-per-column semantics.

SAN preserves a nested-array hypothesis: relatively stable object and body relations provide
context while changing partial inputs produce receiver-relevant updates; repeated
receive-transform-project cycles bind those changes into perception, prediction, and action.
Laminar and multi-area recordings during controlled sensor or object movement can test whether the
predicted stable-and-changing relation is present and causally useful.

## Neural relays, glia, and wider cellular communication

Rank 23 adds a problem that a neuron-only relay cannot solve by itself. Neural signals occur inside
living tissue. Their transmission depends not only on the two neurons most visibly joined by a
synapse, but also on extracellular ions, transmitter clearance, metabolic support, myelination,
immune state, vascular conditions, and structural remodeling.

In plain language, the larger operation is:

```text
specialized receptor converts an external or bodily difference
-> a neural array receives and transforms the resulting relation
-> transient activity and slower structural state shape its output
-> glial, metabolic, ionic, and immune conditions enable or constrain the relay
-> another receiver detects a changed signal
-> the next cycle changes
```

Several experiments establish parts of that operation. Astrocytic calcium elevation changed
transmitter-release probability and plasticity at single hippocampal synapses in a specified
preparation
([Perea and Araque, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1144640)).
Microglial function was required for normal developmental synaptic pruning in a mouse model
([Paolicelli et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1202529)).
Disrupting oligodendroglial MCT1 caused axonal damage and neuronal loss in experimental models,
supporting a distinct metabolic-support role
([Lee et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature11314)).
Outside the nervous system, calcium-oscillation frequency changed the efficiency and selectivity of
transcription-factor and gene-expression responses in T cell populations
([Dolmetsch, Xu, and Lewis, 1998](https://doi.org/10.1038/31960)).

The comparison stops before combining these results into one generic glial or cellular computer.
Astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, immune cells, and neurons remain specialized cell types.
Astrocytic participation varies by circuit and preparation; no universal 40, 80, or 100 percent
tripartite-synapse figure follows. Shared sensing, state change, communication, and adaptation do
not establish identical anatomy, one common code, cellular consciousness, or tomography.

SAN preserves two bounded extensions. NAPOT asks whether repeated neural-array
receive-transform-project cycles can be reconstructed as a distributed, body- and action-relevant
rendering without inserting a hidden viewer. Cellular Oscillating Tomography, or COT, asks whether
temporally structured input-state-output relations across wider cellular arrays can be measured as
nested coordination:

```text
declared cellular input
-> measurable internal temporal state
-> measurable output
-> receiver-specific consequence
-> adaptation of the next cycle
```

Evidence for component interactions makes those questions testable. It does not establish in
advance that non-neural tissue performs neural rendering, cognition, consciousness, or evolutionary
learning. See [[cellular-oscillating-tomography]], [[astrocytes]], [[glial-cells]],
[[cellular-communication-graph-neural-network]], [[cell-as-chemical-neural-network]], and
[[super-evolution-theory]].

## Multimodal and adaptive BCI

Ranks 17, 18, 24, 25, 27, 30, and 32 show a consistent engineering direction:

- EEG supplies fast but spatially ambiguous population measurements;
- fNIRS supplies slower hemodynamic information;
- EOG and EMG can provide robust ocular and motor channels;
- structural and impedance-sensitive imaging constrain anatomy and state;
- stimulation, ultrasound, light, and other write methods require separate safety and efficacy
  evidence;
- behavior and device state close the loop.

The April 2021 hybrid-BCI discussion adds a crucial learning requirement. A decoder trained on an
effortful novice strategy may fail as the user becomes skilled. The interface must follow the
learned operator:

```text
baseline calibration
-> intended action and multimodal measurement
-> device result
-> error and success feedback
-> user learning plus model adaptation
-> longitudinal recalibration
```

This is the bridge among [[neo-mind-cycle-neurofeedback-bci-genealogy]],
[[bci-instrument-atlas]], [[multimodal-neural-state-reconstruction-perturbation]],
[[agency-valuation-action-feedback]], and NerveGear.

### Rank 32: earn the command claim before drawing the boundary

The reviewed eight-command experiment did something real and bounded. Four mental-task classes
were decoded from fNIRS features, four eye-action classes were decoded from an EEG-system branch,
and the two command sets controlled different quadcopter movements
([Khan and Hong, 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbot.2017.00006)).

In plain language:

```text
declared task or eye action
-> measured pattern
-> one of four modality-specific classes
-> declared movement command
-> quadcopter action
```

That is genuine command decoding at the level declared by the training protocol. The branches
were processed independently, so the architecture increased the command vocabulary through
complementary channels rather than one feature-fused eight-class model.

Only after the operation is clear does the semantic boundary become useful. A finite task class
is not yet open-ended thought, detailed content, qualia, selfhood, or conscious-state decoding.
Those are different target levels, not reasons to dismiss the command-level result.

The discussion also requires an attribution repair. Micah's identified or strongly linked
contributions include accuracy versus command vocabulary, acquisition versus fusion,
ocular-versus-cortical labeling, multiple measurement clocks, sampling versus usable bandwidth,
hands-on EEG experience, and source-imaging questions. Sharina's named turns contribute the
novice-to-skilled analogy, effort versus outcome, behavioral feedback, EMG complementarity,
CTRL-Labs comparison, and attention-condition question. Other speakers and the primary paper
remain separate.

The attention question is preserved constructively. Attention, arousal, fatigue, medication,
strategy, and individual physiology may alter measured distributions and decoder performance.
That motivates participant-specific longitudinal measurement; it does not support a universal
statement that one clinical group has "noisier signals."

## Reaching neural traffic while limiting injury

Rank 26 frames interface access as a constrained routing problem. A device must get close enough
to a useful signal or stimulation target while limiting tissue injury, infection, vascular risk,
loss of specificity, instability, and irreversible change.

In plain language, each route trades one cost for another:

```text
target neural operation
-> candidate surface, vascular, peripheral, intranasal, or penetrating route
-> achievable read/write specificity
-> short- and long-term biological risk
-> reversibility, stability, and maintenance
-> bounded human capability
```

An endovascular stent-electrode array has recorded motor-cortical field activity and supported
multiple command control in an early two-participant feasibility study
([Oxley et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1136/neurintsurg-2020-016862)). In a different domain,
intranasally administered molecular tracers reached brain-associated perivascular spaces in rats
([Lochhead et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/jcbfm.2014.215)).

The comparison stops before treating molecular nose-to-brain transport as evidence that a mobile
device can navigate to the human thalamus, establish a stable interface, decode natural content,
or stimulate safely. The endovascular result is also a bounded motor-command readout, not a
full-brain read/write system.

SAN preserves the intranasal nanorobot as early NerveGear invention genealogy and retains the
broader design rule: compare routes by the neural operation reached, specificity, reversibility,
stability, biological response, and demonstrated capability. A future implementation must earn
each link experimentally.

## Microtubules and Orch OR

Rank 31 begins with an ordinary biological question: if microtubules do more than hold a neuron's
shape and move cargo, how could their changing state become information the rest of the neuron can
use?

The proposed operation is:

```text
intracellular microtubule state
-> a cellular structure responds
-> membrane, channel, transport, or plasticity state changes
-> dendritic integration, spiking, or synaptic output changes
-> a population-level representation, memory, choice, or action changes
```

Several established or measured findings make that question legitimate. Dendritic microtubules
have mixed polarity ([Baas et al., 1988](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.85.21.8335)).
Dynamic microtubule ends can enter dendritic spines and influence spine morphology and
plasticity-related organization
([Jaworski et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2008.11.013);
[Hu et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2445-11.2011)).
Prepared rat-brain microtubule bundles generated electrical oscillations, often with a 39 Hz
fundamental, and membrane-permeabilized cultured neurites transmitted related cytoskeletal
signals ([Cantero et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-30453-2)).

Orch OR supplies a more specific comparison theory: quantum states in microtubules are proposed
to be orchestrated and reduced as conscious moments. Later Hameroff work proposes nested
microtubule resonances and mixed-polarity dendritic-somatic arrays
([Hameroff, 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnmol.2022.869935)). That comparison is scientifically
useful because both programs ask how intracellular dynamics could reach larger neural scales.

The comparison stops before treating a 39 Hz preparation result as the source of scalp gamma,
cytoskeletal disruption as proof that memory is stored in microtubules, or a proposed
quantum-collapse mechanism as established neural physiology. The 2021 SAN source already states
the additive rule: a confirmed microtubule process would extend, not erase, LTP, LTD, protein
synthesis, recurrent activity, and circuit learning.

SAN preserves the scale-translation hypothesis. Microtubules may contribute to a larger
intracellular-to-dendritic-to-network process, but the candidate observer remains the distributed
system that receives, transforms, reexpresses, remembers, chooses, acts, and receives the
consequences. The decisive test is to perturb a declared microtubule state in living neural tissue
and measure the predicted cellular reader, membrane or plasticity effect, circuit consequence, and
behavioral or conscious target. See [[microtubule-to-circuit-scale-translation]] and
[[microtubules-anesthesia-and-orch-or-evidence-boundary]].
