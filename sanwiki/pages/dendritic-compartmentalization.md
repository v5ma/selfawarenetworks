Dendritic compartmentalization is the property of neurons whereby individual dendritic
branches act as semi-independent computational units, each capable of integrating
synaptic inputs and generating local electrical events (plateau potentials, calcium
spikes) that influence — but do not fully determine — the somatic [[action-potential|action potential]].

In SAN theory, this property is foundational: a single neuron is not a simple
threshold gate but a **miniature neural network** in its own right. The [[dendrite]]
partitions contextual inputs into voltage compartments, enabling the cell to
[[multiplex]] distinct signal streams simultaneously.

## Key mechanisms

- **Calcium signaling**: dendritic calcium transients gate [[neuroplasticity]] locally;
  only synapses within an active compartment are eligible for potentiation.
- **Plateau potentials**: soma-dendritic interactions produce sustained depolarizations
  that extend the effective integration window far beyond a single spike.
- **Spatial organization**: the physical layout of synaptic inputs along the dendritic
  arbor encodes context — nearby co-active inputs are treated as a conjunctive feature.

## Human vs. rodent distinctions

Human cortical neurons are substantially larger than rodent equivalents, with
proportionally more dendritic length and ion-channel distribution spread across more
compartments. The hypothesis from this source: "perhaps the increasing computational
power in the human cortex is less about the number of neurons — and more about the
number of compartments."

This aligns with SAN's claim that [[self-aware-network]] complexity scales with
intra-neuron representational capacity, not simply synapse count.

## Connection to neuromodulation

[[Inhibitory-circuits]] and neuromodulators (dopamine, serotonin) interact with
dendritic compartments to gate which inputs are integrated at any moment — a dynamic
context filter that shapes what the neuron "notices." This gating is central to
[[attention]] as modeled in SAN.
