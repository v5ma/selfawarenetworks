# Holographic Brain Model

The **holographic brain model** is a historical family of analogies and hypotheses in which distributed neural activity is compared with holographic storage or reconstruction. SAN uses parts of this imagery to discuss partial views, interference, distributed memory, and reconstruction. The analogy is not established storage anatomy, and the brain is not known to implement an optical hologram.

## What Is Established

Memory and perception are distributed across interacting cells and regions, but they are also selective, structured, and task-dependent. Individual neurons and populations can be tuned to features, locations, relations, concepts, actions, or combinations of variables. Engram studies show that manipulating selected cell populations can alter later recall in bounded preparations. Population-level semantic and perceptual geometry can be distributed without every neuron or tissue fragment containing the complete represented object.

Representative component evidence includes [Gelbard-Sagiv and colleagues' single-neuron recall study](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1164685), [Liu and colleagues' optogenetic engram experiment](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature11028), and [Huth and colleagues' distributed semantic-map study](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature17637).

These findings support distributed and reconstructive organization. They do not establish holographic interference storage, equal memory distribution across cortex, or complete-object information in every local region.

## Historical Lineage

Karl Lashley's lesion work motivated debates about localization and distributed contribution. Karl Pribram later developed a holonomic or holographic brain proposal. Modern distributed-representation, attractor, predictive, reference-frame, and population-coding theories address related problems using different mechanisms.

Historical importance does not make the optical analogy literal. Lesion effects depend on task, site, extent, plasticity, and remaining pathways; they cannot be summarized as “removing cortex only makes every memory hazy.”

## SAN and NAPOT Interpretation

SAN proposes a different, testable reconstruction question:

> Can multiple partial, time-varying neural projections be identified, integrated into a working latent state, and redistributed so that perception, memory, and action update together?

That is closer to recurrent distributed reconstruction than to a claim that every point stores the whole image. In the SAN interpretation:

- **tonic activity** is the receiver-relative ongoing context or canvas;
- **phasic activity** supplies differentiated changes or “ink” relative to that context;
- learned dendritic, cellular, and circuit structure constrains what is detected and re-expressed;
- recurrent projections update distributed population states; and
- action and reafferent consequences test and modify the next state.

The network itself performs these operations. No reference beam illuminates a hidden screen for an inner viewer.

## Scale Boundaries

| Scale | Defensible claim | Unsupported shortcut |
|---|---|---|
| Micro | A neuron integrates structured synaptic input and projects an output that affects downstream receivers | One neuron stores a complete 3D viewpoint or conscious pixel |
| Meso | Assemblies and local circuits can complete, transform, or route learned patterns | Every cortical column implements the same reconstruction algorithm |
| Macro | Distributed recurrent networks can maintain and update task-relevant world and body states | The whole brain is one literal optical hologram |

The mechanism cannot be assumed to repeat unchanged at every scale. A cellular coincidence relation, a circuit attractor, and a systems-level reconstruction have different variables and evidence.

## Relation to the Thousand Brains Theory

The Thousand Brains Theory proposes multiple cortical reference-frame models and voting across columns. It is a useful comparator, not evidence that each column stores an identical copy or that SAN's oscillatory carrier is established. Any comparison should specify the represented variable, coordinate frame, transformation, voting or routing operation, and behavioral test.

## What Would Test a Holographic-Like SAN Claim?

A strong experiment would need to:

1. identify multiple partial projections and their information limits;
2. show held-out reconstruction beyond flexible recurrent and latent-state baselines;
3. perturb one projection while preserving total input where possible;
4. predict a selective reconstruction or action error;
5. rescue the error by restoring the declared relation; and
6. measure how degradation scales with the removed information.

Graceful degradation alone is insufficient because redundancy, attractor completion, distributed coding, and model capacity can produce it without holographic storage.

## Status

- **Established:** distributed representation, selective engram participation, recurrent completion, and population-level representational geometry in bounded preparations.
- **Historical hypothesis:** holonomic or holographic descriptions of neural storage and perception.
- **SAN proposal:** recurrent partial-projection reconstruction integrated with tonic context, phasic differentiation, neural tuning, and observer-action.
- **Not established:** literal neural holography, one complete viewpoint per neuron, universal scale repetition, or holographic reconstruction as consciousness.
