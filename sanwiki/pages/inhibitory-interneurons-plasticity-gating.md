Inhibitory interneurons act as dual gatekeepers of synaptic plasticity: soma-targeting
parvalbumin-positive cells control global excitability and spike timing, while
[[dendrite]]-targeting somatostatin-positive cells filter specific inputs at the dendritic
level, together determining *when and where* synaptic modification is permitted.

## Core mechanism

The two interneuron classes partition gating authority: parvalbumin cells enforce a
whole-cell go/no-go on firing, somatostatin cells enforce compartment-local gates on
dendritic integration. Plasticity requires both gates to be open simultaneously —
a [[temporal-spatial-coincidence-detection]] requirement that generalises Hebbian rules
into the behavioral time-scale plasticity (BTSP) regime.

## Multi-scale coincidence detection

The gating logic nests across scales. The same coincidence-detection principle that
operates within a single dendritic spine also operates at local circuit, cortical column,
and distributed-network levels simultaneously. This resonates with SAN's emphasis on
[[fractal-pattern]] repetition of information-processing motifs across scales
([[napot]] predicts exactly this kind of scale-recursive oscillatory structure).

Learning (coincidence → synaptic strengthening) and unlearning (oscillatory decoherence
→ synaptic weakening) are posited to run concurrently at all four levels, not
sequentially.

## Clinical relevance

Disruption of the parvalbumin-cell layer contributes to epilepsy (excess excitability),
schizophrenia (spike-timing desynchrony), and autism spectrum conditions (impaired
[[dendrite]]-level filtering). Each pathology can be understood as a failure of a specific
gating layer rather than a global network collapse.

## History

Classical Hebbian plasticity assumed coincident pre- and post-synaptic firing as the
sole gate. BTSP and the dual-interneuron model extend this to multi-compartment,
multi-timescale gating, resolving anomalies where Hebbian rules failed to account for
behavioral-scale learning.
