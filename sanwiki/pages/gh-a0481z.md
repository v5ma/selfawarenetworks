# Matrices, Morphisms, and Neural State Change (a0481z)

## Historical source

This short note tried to connect three ways of representing a neural association:

> The matrix is the arrow or morphism between points or objects which are vectors. The matrix represents associations in the brain, like a synapse-dendrite association. In Gothendeick topology the matrix is the arrow or morphism between objects (points, sets, names) which are the vectors inside these matrices.

"Gothendeick" is almost certainly a spelling or transcription error for **Grothendieck**. The
surrounding SAN sources show that the intended subject was category theory, vector geometry,
relationships among neural states, and composition across biological and artificial processing
paths. The note should not be read as a formal definition of a Grothendieck topology.

## The mathematical distinctions

The original sentence becomes useful when its compressed concepts are separated:

1. **Graph relation:** a synaptic contact can be represented as a directed edge from a presynaptic
   source to a postsynaptic compartment.
2. **Matrix representation:** a connectivity or weight matrix can summarize many declared
   relations. A matrix can represent a linear map only after the domain, codomain, variables, and
   bases are specified.
3. **State transformation:** a neuron's or population's next measured state can be modeled as a
   transformation of its current inputs and state.
4. **Categorical morphism:** category theory can represent a declared transformation as an arrow
   between declared objects, with composition rules that must be defined.
5. **Grothendieck topology:** this is a further mathematical structure that specifies covering
   families or covering sieves on a category. Nothing in the short note yet defines those covers.

A synapse is therefore not literally a matrix, vector, or morphism. Those are different models of
anatomy, measured activity, or transformation.

## A medically bounded neural model

Let `x(t)` be a vector of declared neural measurements, `W` a declared connectivity or coupling
estimate, `u(t)` an input vector, and `s(t)` the receiver's membrane, conductance, inhibition, and
recent-history state. A deliberately simplified model is:

The modeled update is `x(t + dt)` = `F(x(t), W, u(t), s(t), noise)`.

Only when the approximation is linear over a declared regime does this reduce toward:

The regime-limited approximation is `x(t + dt)` approximately `W x(t) + B u(t)`.

Real dendritic integration can be branch-specific, nonlinear, history-dependent, and
state-dependent. A connectivity matrix also does not by itself specify which routes are active,
how synaptic efficacy changes, or what a population state means for a task.

## Recovered SAN operation

The 2012-2021 source family develops the following proposal:

- **Biological path:** measured neural or sensory state -> declared biological transformation ->
  new population state or action.
- **Artificial path:** measured artificial sensor state -> declared computational transformation ->
  comparable model output or action.

Category-theoretic diagrams could test whether the two paths preserve a declared relationship or
composition. They must not be called isomorphic merely because they produce one similar output.
The state variables, maps, composition law, interventions, and tolerated equivalence all need to
be specified.

## Scientific status

### Established components

- Neural population activity is routinely represented as a vector and studied as a trajectory
  through a measured state space.
- Connectivity and coupling can be represented with matrices, with assumptions and uncertainty.
- Thin dendritic branches can perform spatially localized nonlinear integration.
- Recurrent neural populations can implement context-dependent transformations that are more
  informative at the population level than one-cell labels.

### Supported interpretation

Graph, matrix, dynamical-systems, and category-theoretic descriptions can emphasize different
parts of the same experiment: anatomy, approximate coupling, measured state change, and
composition. They are complementary mathematical views, not interchangeable biological objects.

### SAN hypothesis

SAN proposes that a composition-preserving description can connect receiver-specific cellular
transformations, population trajectories, array-to-array transformations, and action-return
loops. A stronger cross-substrate claim would require biological and artificial paths to preserve
the same preregistered relations under new inputs and perturbations, not merely imitate one output.

No vector, matrix, category, or morphism is a hidden observer. The measured states belong to the
distributed perceiving and acting system.

## Scale check

- **Micro:** synapse, dendritic branch, soma, and axon initial segment; model as directed contacts
  plus a nonlinear receiver transformation.
- **Meso:** local recurrent population; model as a connectivity or coupling estimate plus a
  population-state trajectory.
- **Macro:** distributed sensory, association, memory, and action systems; model as a composed
  pathway with anatomy, delays, body state, and task variables.
- **Cross-substrate:** biological and artificial experimental paths; compare with a commutative or
  equivalence test over declared observables.

## Decisive test

1. Define the biological and artificial input, intermediate state, and output objects.
2. Define every map, its measured variables, uncertainty, and allowed composition.
3. Train or fit both paths on one set of inputs.
4. Test held-out inputs and preregistered perturbations.
5. Compare full trajectories and receiver-specific consequences, not only endpoint labels.
6. Reject the claimed equivalence if the diagram fails to commute within the declared tolerance
   or if a simpler non-categorical model predicts the results equally well.
