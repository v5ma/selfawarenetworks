# Dendritic Spines

**Dendritic spines** are small protrusions from dendrites that receive many excitatory synapses in the mammalian brain. A spine head and narrow neck can partially compartmentalize voltage, calcium, receptors, signaling proteins, and cytoskeletal remodeling. Spines are important sites of synaptic transmission and plasticity, not self-contained memory bits.

## Synaptic microdomains

At a typical glutamatergic spine synapse, AMPA receptors carry much of the fast excitatory current, while NMDA receptors contribute voltage-dependent current and calcium entry. Calcium can also enter through voltage-gated channels and can be released from intracellular stores. The amount, timing, and location of calcium help recruit signaling pathways that alter receptor trafficking, actin, local protein interactions, and later synaptic efficacy.

Spine shape matters, but not as a simple code. Neck geometry can affect electrical and biochemical coupling to the parent dendrite, while receptor composition, presynaptic release, neighboring inhibition, and dendritic voltage also shape the response. Some excitatory synapses occur on shafts, and some inhibitory synapses occur on spines, so the usual diagram is not a universal wiring rule.

## Plasticity and memory

[Matsuzaki et al. (2004)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature02617) used two-photon glutamate uncaging at individual hippocampal CA1 spines. Stimulated spines enlarged and showed increased AMPA-receptor-mediated current; persistent enlargement in small spines required NMDA receptors, calmodulin, actin polymerization, and CaMKII. This supports input-specific coupling between structural and functional plasticity in that preparation.

It does not follow that every enlarged spine is a complete long-term memory, that LTD always prunes a spine, or that spine size alone specifies synaptic strength. Memory-related changes are distributed across many synapses, cells, circuits, intrinsic conductances, and systems-level processes.

## SAN interpretation

SAN can use the spine as a **local biochemical state variable** within a larger receive-integrate-project system. A spine can change how one synaptic contact contributes to later dendritic and cellular activity. The network, not an isolated spine, supplies the distributed context that gives the event functional meaning.

Tonic and phasic terminology should remain an interpretation: ongoing membrane and network state can condition a transient synaptic event, but one calcium transient is not automatically "phasic ink" and a spine is not a pixel with a fixed semantic label. The claim becomes testable when the page specifies the measured baseline, transient event, synapse, branch, cell output, and behavioral consequence.
