## Post-SAN

Mainstream information-theoretic neuroscience (efficient-coding, sparse-coding, predictive-coding) already commits to a version of the rarity principle: the brain spends more representational resources on rare events than on common ones, and predictive-coding circuits suppress *expected* signals to expose *unexpected* ones. SAN's reformulation makes two specific commitments worth flagging against the published literature. First, it predicts that the *substrate* signature of high-rarity events should be a *phase-discontinuity* in [[cortical-traveling-waves|cortical traveling waves]] — measurable in [[phase-wave-differentials|phase-wave differentials]] read out by [[napot-neural-array-phase-tomography|NAPOT]] — rather than only a firing-rate elevation. Second, by binding the rarity principle to [[neural-anti-bit|the neural-anti-bit]] dual construct, SAN predicts that the *suppression* of expected coincidences is itself an active, measurable substrate-level event, not a passive baseline — i.e. high-rarity events are *exposed* by the brain's continuous suppression of low-rarity ones, and that suppression should be observable. Where SAN converges with the mainstream is in [[integrated-information-theory|integrated information theory]]'s commitment to information-as-difference and in the predictive-coding literature's commitment to surprise-driven learning. Where it diverges is in the *substrate-measurement* level at which the rarity principle is supposed to operate.
