# Cognitive Liberty

## The Problem Before The Term

A brain-computer system can do more than collect a signal. It can measure neural or bodily
activity, transform that measurement, infer something about the person, choose a response, and
then change what the person sees, hears, feels, or receives through stimulation.

```text
activity in a person
-> measurement
-> signal transformation
-> inference about state or intention
-> device or institutional decision
-> feedback, adaptation, or intervention
-> the person's next state and action
```

Each arrow creates a different question. Did the person consent to the measurement? Is the
inference accurate? Is it being used for the agreed purpose? Can the person inspect, interrupt, or
reverse the intervention? Who retains the signal, the inference, and the resulting model?

**Cognitive liberty** names the right at the center of those questions: a person should retain
meaningful control over whether and how a technology measures, infers, influences, or writes into
their mental life. **Mental privacy** applies that principle to neural data and to inferences about
thought, intention, emotion, memory, or other mental states.

## Self-Direction Is An Operation, Not A Hidden Observer

People can sometimes notice a thought or image, sustain it, redirect it, compare it with an
alternative, or allow it to fade. In plain language, the brain is operating on its own activity.
This does not require a little viewer or executive hidden inside the brain. Monitoring and control
can be distributed across the same embodied system that generates perception, memory, valuation,
and action.

Research on metacognition supports a distinction between performing a task and judging one's own
performance. Research on voluntary imagery shows that internally generated content can affect
measurable sensory representations. Closed-loop experiments have also demonstrated limited,
trained control over decoded semantic dimensions. These findings establish bounded forms of
self-monitoring, imagery, and neural feedback; they do not establish unrestricted access to
private thought or a single neural orchestrator.

## What Established Research And Policy Add

Ienca and Andorno distinguish cognitive liberty, mental privacy, mental integrity, and
psychological continuity as related but non-identical neurorights. Their cognitive-liberty account
includes both access to neurotechnology and freedom from compelled use.

The OECD's responsible-neurotechnology recommendation addresses brain data, consent, individual
choice, unauthorized use, discrimination, oversight, and responsible innovation. UNESCO's
Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology extends the governance problem to direct and
indirect inference of mental states, autonomy, freedom of thought, purpose limitation, data
minimization, manipulation, closed-loop systems, workplaces, and extended-reality settings.

This policy literature supports a rights-and-governance framework. It does not prove SAN's account
of consciousness, Neural Rendering, or any proposed neural code.

The capability evidence is also graded. Neuralink's 2019 white paper described a
high-channel-count research platform and implant prototype. A 2021 intracortical study decoded
attempted handwriting movements from one participant's motor-cortex activity. A 2023 fMRI study
reconstructed aspects of perceived and imagined language with participant-specific training and
reported that cooperation was required for successful training and use. These are meaningful
readout operations, but each declares a task, instrument, participant, training procedure,
decoder, and output. None is an unrestricted open-brain reader.

## Where The Comparison Stops

Established work supports metacognitive report, voluntary imagery effects, limited decoded
feedback control, and a serious governance need for neural data and mental-state inference. It
does not by itself establish:

- complete or reliable decoding of a person's thoughts;
- arbitrary writing of experiences, memories, or intentions;
- one oscillatory signature for self-direction;
- one brain region that acts as the self;
- a legal right with identical force in every jurisdiction; or
- the full SAN receive-transform-project account of consciousness.

Raw neural data, a model's inference, an operator's interpretation, and a device's intervention
must therefore remain separate evidence objects.

## The SAN Extension

SAN extends the established pieces into a testable design hypothesis. A future read/write
interface should protect the whole closed loop, not only the original recording. This is the
**read-transform-infer-write-return loop**:

```text
person-originated activity
-> measured signal
-> transformed representation
-> inferred state
-> selected response
-> feedback or stimulation
-> returned bodily and neural consequence
```

Under this proposal, cognitive liberty is not satisfied by a one-time checkbox. It should be
measurable in the architecture:

1. the user can tell when the loop is active;
2. each inference and intervention has a declared purpose;
3. the user can refuse, pause, or end the loop;
4. effects are reversible where technically possible;
5. raw signals and inferred states have separate access controls;
6. the system records who initiated each read, inference, and write;
7. safety limits constrain harmful outputs without silently transferring ownership of the
   person's mental activity; and
8. the device's adaptation remains inspectable enough to distinguish assistance from covert
   manipulation.

SAN further hypothesizes that self-direction can be studied as a distributed control trajectory:
which representation is maintained, which alternative is suppressed or selected, what action
follows, and how returned consequences change the next state. That proposal belongs with
[[agency-valuation-action-feedback]], [[body-model-as-control-surface]],
[[artificial-neurology-states-stages-development]], and
[[mechanistic-emergence-model-adequacy]]. It remains a hypothesis until its variables and tests
are implemented.
