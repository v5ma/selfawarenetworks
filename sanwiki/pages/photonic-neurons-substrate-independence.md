The question of whether intelligence requires biological neurons, or whether any substrate exhibiting equivalent computational properties — including photonic (light-based) systems — could support mind.

## Core question

If neurons were replaced by fiber-optic cables and action potentials by beams of light, would intelligence be preserved? The answer turns on substrate independence: what matters for cognition is the *functional organization* — the pattern of signal propagation, integration, and modification — not the physical medium (electrons vs. photons).

## Photonic computing

Photonic chips use lasers to transmit information. Early 2012 results showed programmable quantum photonic processors capable of executing algorithms. The question for SAN is whether such systems could replicate the oscillatory, phase-coherent properties of biological [[neural-oscillation-hierarchy|neural oscillations]] that appear to be central to consciousness in the [[napot]] framework.

## The synaptic constraint

One objection: synaptic weights depend on biochemical processes (receptor binding, vesicle release) that have no direct photonic analog. Without a mechanism for light-triggered synaptic modification, a photonic neuron array might lack the plasticity that grounds learning and [[gh-a0206z|self]]-modeling.

## Significance for SAN

[[napot]] argues that the *pattern* of oscillatory phase relationships — not the carrier medium — produces [[qualia]] and [[self-modeling]] in biological brains. If correct, a photonic system that reproduces those oscillatory patterns would be conscious. Substrate independence is thus a direct corollary of the NAPOT theory.
