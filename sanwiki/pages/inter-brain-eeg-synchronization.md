Inter-brain EEG synchronization is the observed convergence of [[tonic-oscillation]] frequencies between two individuals during conversation, measurable in the EEG spectrum. The phenomenon suggests that communication is *enabled by* phase-locking onto a shared wavelength — not merely correlated with it.

In the SAN framework, this is the inter-module communication protocol operating *between* brains, analogous to the synchrony that links [[cortical-columns]] within a single brain. Two people talking are not just exchanging words; they are briefly becoming a distributed two-node [[self-aware-network]].

## Proposed experiment

[[gh-resnote04|Micah]] Blumberg]] proposed a six-subject study to characterize the synchrony [[attractor]]:

- Three subjects pre-entrained to **alpha** states (bright light, coffee, alertness)
- Three subjects pre-entrained to **delta** states (binaural/isochronic beats, dim light)
- All pairings tested (alpha×alpha, delta×delta, alpha×delta) via rotating conversation blocks
- Outcome: which frequency wins — the dominant, the more alert, or the arithmetic median?

The equilibrium question is the core finding: does synchrony converge at a mean (theta/beta), does delta pull alpha down, or does alpha pull delta up?

## Connection to distributed cognition

The [[brain-as-fractal-of-modules]] architecture means each skull is one semi-autonomous node. The octopus — multiple distributed nervous systems operating as one organism — shows that modularity plus communication can produce a unified agent without a single center. Inter-brain synchrony during speech hints that sufficiently coupled humans may briefly exhibit shared cognitive states, a precondition for [[distributed-consciousness]].

This line of reasoning feeds directly into SAN's model of [[conscious-robot]] design: an artificial network of nodes that can achieve synchrony across physical boundaries would exhibit the same inter-module coherence observed in biological conversation.
