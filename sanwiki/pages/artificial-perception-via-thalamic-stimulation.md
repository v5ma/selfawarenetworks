Artificial Perception via Thalamic Stimulation is a candidate SAN experiment family, not an established universal route for writing perception. It asks whether precisely controlled intervention in a modality-specific thalamic circuit can produce or alter a bounded percept in a way predicted by SAN's timing and state hypotheses.

## Why the thalamus is relevant

Thalamic nuclei participate in sensory relay, cortical state, attention, recurrent corticothalamic loops, and motor-related pathways. Their topographic and circuit-specific organization makes some nuclei plausible intervention targets for bounded sensory questions.

The thalamus is not one uniform gateway. Olfaction differs from the classic relay pattern; individual nuclei have distinct inputs and outputs; and cortical, subcortical, peripheral, and brainstem intervention routes may be more appropriate for other questions.

## The SAN-specific question

Ordinary stimulation experiments can test whether activating a target changes perception. The stronger SAN test asks whether receiver-relative timing, waveform, magnitude, and ongoing state predict the result beyond target location and mean activation alone.

A useful comparison would hold the stimulated population and total delivered energy as constant as practical while varying a preregistered timing relation. SAN predicts a specific pattern of perceptual or behavioral change only if the proposed phase relation is causally relevant to the receiving circuit.

## Minimum experiment specification

1. Name the sensory quality or discrimination being tested.
2. Name the thalamic nucleus, projection, cell class, and relevant cortical target.
3. Define the measured baseline and the proposed relative timing variable.
4. Specify waveform, duration, amplitude, spatial pattern, and intervention limits.
5. Include rate-, energy-, location-, expectation-, and state-matched controls.
6. Measure behavior, confidence, physiology, adverse effects, and recovery.
7. State what result would count against the SAN timing hypothesis.

## What success would and would not show

A specific, reproducible induced percept would show causal access to one sensory circuit. If phase-aware stimulation outperformed matched controls as predicted, it would support a bounded SAN claim about timing in that circuit.

It would not establish a universal thalamic code, complete multimodal perception, the sufficiency of NAPOT for consciousness, or direct transfer of one person's percept into another person's brain.

## Current evidence posture

The earlier auto-generated page reported an evidence count of zero for its specific thalamic phase-injection mechanism. The general relevance of thalamic circuits comes from established neuroanatomy and stimulation research, but that background must not be presented as direct validation of the stronger SAN protocol. A literature and experiment packet remains required before this design can move beyond hypothesis status.

## Safety boundary

This is an experiment-design concept, not a clinical protocol. Human thalamic intervention is invasive and requires medical, ethical, regulatory, and informed-consent oversight. Noninvasive, animal, computational, or already clinically indicated recording opportunities should be evaluated before proposing new invasive exposure.
