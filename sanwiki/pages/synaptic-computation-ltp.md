**Synaptic computation** refers to the enormous information-processing capacity concentrated at individual synapses — not just as relay nodes but as active computational units with over 1,000 distinct proteins per synapse combining to produce variable post-synaptic responses.

A single synapse is influenced by: (a) the pre-synaptic neuron's spiking history, (b) its own post-synaptic receptor configuration, (c) the surrounding cerebrospinal fluid carrying neurotransmitters, hormones, and EM fields. This three-way context makes each synapse individually adaptive, not a passive wire.

This page-specific image frames the claim as a three-step mechanism: local context sets the synaptic state, the active synapse computes a variable postsynaptic response, and repeated timing can consolidate the changed response into structural LTP.

## Long-term potentiation (LTP) and structural change

Short-term memory may reside in volatile synaptic representations (current receptor states). Long-term memory requires **structural consolidation**: synaptogenesis, dendritic spine formation, dendritic arborization, and in some cases neurogenesis. These structural changes are induced by repeated temporal spiking patterns.

[[gh-resnote04|Micah]]'s contribution in the early SAN notes: long-term memory also requires the **formation of new proteins** that migrate to the neuron's nucleus, altering its physical shape. Specifically, **CaMKII** (calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II) interacts with [[microtubules]] in a manner likened to a six-bit "hammer" that fires each time the synapse activates — a molecular-scale encoding mechanism that anchors memory into the cytoskeleton.

## Scale hierarchy

- **Synapse**: ~1,000 proteins, volatile computation.
- **Neuron**: tens of thousands of synaptic connections + [[dendritic-computation|dendritic computation]] + soma integration.
- **Cortical column**: ~10,000 neurons, a network within a node.
- **Brain**: small-world architecture with ≤6 degrees of separation between any two neurons.

This nested hierarchy is foundational to [[napot]]: each level detects and projects patterns, with the column level being a key unit of [[neural-array-projection]].

## SDRs and HTM

Sparse Distributed Representations ([[sparse-distributed-representations]]) encode information efficiently in mostly-zero bitstreams. While useful for Jeff Hawkins's HTM model, they are a mathematical construct, not a direct model of neuroscience — both views are noted as partial and compatible.
