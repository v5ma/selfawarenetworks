(all or nothing)(physics)

This page records a conjecture about whether inverse relationships between frequency and magnitude can be used as a cross-scale analogy between brain activity, neuronal action potentials, and larger physical wave systems. The idea should be read as a hypothesis, not as an established account of general relativity, quantum physics, or neuroscience.

The source text starts from the mass-energy relation and asks whether a future theory connecting general relativity and quantum physics could be framed in terms of relationships among mass, energy, frequency, and wave magnitude. In this repaired version, that physics claim is kept as a motivating analogy rather than a settled scientific statement.

The neuroscience portion of the conjecture compares three scales. At the EEG scale, neural field activity often shows frequency-dependent power structure, including 1/f-like or scale-free components. At the single-neuron scale, action potentials are commonly treated as threshold events with relatively stereotyped peak amplitude, following the all-or-none framing formalized in conductance-based models. At the synaptic scale, spike timing, waveform duration, and ion-channel dynamics can affect downstream release probability and timing.

The proposed hypothesis is that when amplitude does not vary enough to explain a frequency change, duration or waveform shape may carry part of the magnitude change. In this framing, an action potential with similar peak amplitude could still differ in duration, after-hyperpolarization, or calcium-channel opening time, which could affect neurotransmitter release. This is a plausible mechanistic question in neuroscience, but the page does not establish that the same inverse relationship applies at every physical scale.

For groups of oscillating neurons, the source contrasts power-band activity such as delta, theta, alpha, beta, gamma, and high gamma with the single-neuron all-or-none principle. The key question is how population-level amplitude or power changes relate to single-neuron spike timing, synchronization, recruitment, and waveform dynamics. The cited neuroscience literature supports the narrower claim that brain rhythms coordinate neural assemblies and that brain activity can show scale-free or 1/f-like structure; it does not prove the universal scale-invariance conjecture.

The informal analogy in the source compares fast, weak events with slower, stronger events. Restated more carefully, the conjecture is that some systems trade frequency against another measure of magnitude, such as amplitude, duration, power, or integrated effect. The page leaves this as an open hypothesis requiring mathematical definition and empirical tests.

Neurons are both convergent and divergent signal processors: they integrate incoming synaptic inputs and distribute outputs through axonal branches. If spike timing or waveform duration changes the amount or timing of neurotransmitter release, then a population-level signal could be shaped by both the number of participating neurons and the temporal structure of their spikes.

## Research Notes

- Hodgkin and Huxley's 1952 model remains a foundational source for conductance-based descriptions of action-potential initiation and propagation: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12991237/
- Buzsaki and Draguhn's 2004 review supports the claim that neuronal oscillations coordinate neural assemblies across frequency bands: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15218136/
- He (2014) supports the narrower claim that brain activity at several measurement scales can show 1/f-like or scale-free structure: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4149861/
