*Toxoplasma gondii* is a parasitic organism that can infect the human brain and alter neurochemistry without the host's awareness, producing measurable changes in behavior and increasing risk of psychosis, schizophrenia, and suicide — even in individuals with no prior psychiatric history.

## Mechanism in SAN terms

Within the SAN framework, behavior is the output of a deterministic neural information-processing system (see [[napot]]). Toxoplasmosis demonstrates that this system can be hijacked at the biochemical level: the parasite modifies the neurochemical milieu — particularly dopamine pathways — in ways that shift the phase and amplitude of [[neural-oscillations|neural oscillations]] governing threat assessment, risk-taking, and affect regulation. The infected brain does not "choose" altered behavior; it outputs altered behavior because its substrate has changed.

This is a concrete instantiation of the principle articulated in [[neuroscience-moral-responsibility]]: biological factors can cause harmful behavior without engaging volitional agency in any meaningful moral sense.

## Epidemiological evidence

Studies linking *T. gondii* infection to psychiatric outcomes include:
- Elevated suicide risk in previously unaffected individuals
- Increased incidence of schizophrenia spectrum symptoms
- Impulsivity and reduced latent inhibition, consistent with dopamine dysregulation

These findings suggest the parasite effectively re-tunes the brain's oscillatory defaults — altering how sensory input is weighted and how responses are generated — without any damage that would be visible as a gross lesion.

## Why this matters for moral theory

If a person commits violence while harboring an undiagnosed *T. gondii* infection that has altered their dopamine dynamics and threat sensitivity, the standard legal framework of culpable [[choice]] is undermined. [[gh-resnote04|Micah]]'s 2012 position: cases of this type will eventually require courts to treat behavior as a medical output rather than a moral failure — merging judicial and clinical responses into a diagnostics-first model.

See [[agental-morality]] for the SAN account of how morality is grounded in internal goal representations, and [[neuroscience-moral-responsibility]] for the broader argument that evil may be a treatable medical condition.
