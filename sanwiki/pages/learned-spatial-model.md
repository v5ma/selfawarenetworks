# Learned Spatial Model

**Learned Spatial Model** is the [[self-aware-networks|Self-Aware Networks]] term for the *internal, experience-built representation of space* that a brain (or a brain-style agent) constructs from sensory and motor history — the cortical analogue of a SLAM-style map and a generalisation of the Hawkins/Mountcastle [[reference-frames-cortical-knowledge|reference-frame]] story. It is the layer of representation that sits between low-level sensory features and high-level [[virtual-models-consciousness|virtual models of consciousness]], and it is what [[grid-cells-place-cells-oscillators|grid cells, place cells, and the oscillators that coordinate them]] are taken to *implement* in the SAN graph. ## Position

A *learned spatial model* in the SAN sense is not a single map but a *layered* representation. At the lowest layer it is built from sensory micro-features — visual edges, vestibular drift, somatosensory contact — that the agent's experience binds into stable feature-conjunctions inside [[cortical-columns-reference-frames|cortical-column reference frames]]. At the next layer up it is the *path-integrated, allocentric* coordinate frame that [[grid-cells-place-cells-oscillators|grid cells and place cells]] deliver from the entorhinal-hippocampal system, with the [[entorhinal-cortex|entorhinal cortex]] supplying the metric scaffolding that the [[hippocampal-reference-frames|hippocampal reference frames]] anchor to landmarks. At the next layer up it is the *multi-timescale* aggregate that [[multi-timescale-reference-frames|multi-timescale reference-frames]] and [[tonic-oscillation-as-predictive-reference-frame|tonic-oscillation-as-predictive-reference-frame]] use to keep predictions coherent across short-, medium-, and long-horizon spatial expectations. At the highest layer it is the *[[gh-a0206z|self]]-locating* frame that [[self-as-navigational-reference-frame|self-as-navigational-reference-frame]] and [[three-tier-spatial-consciousness-model|three-tier-spatial-consciousness-model]] treat as the substrate of phenomenal "where I am" experience. The SAN-internal addition to this stack is that *every layer is implemented in the same oscillatory-phase substrate*: the [[phase-wave-differentials|phase-wave-differential]] across columns *is* the spatial-prediction error, [[neural-ensemble-phase-reset-communication|phase-reset]] is the map-update event, and [[napot-neural-array-phase-tomography|NAPOT]]-style readout is the operator that turns a column's local phase pattern into a contribution to the agent's whole-system spatial estimate.

## Chronology

The mainstream chronology of the *learned spatial model* construct runs from Tolman's 1948 *Cognitive Maps in Rats and Men*, through O'Keefe and Nadel's 1978 *The Hippocampus as a Cognitive Map*, the discovery of grid cells by Hafting/Fyhn/Moser/Moser in 2005 (Nobel 2014), and into Hawkins/Mountcastle-style cortical-reference-frame proposals from the early 2010s onward (Hawkins, Lewis, Klukas et al. 2019). ## Post-SAN

Mainstream cognitive-map and SLAM literature treats the *learned spatial model* as a *representation* problem — what variables encode space, how they are updated from sensory input, how they are used by downstream policy. SAN's commitments push past description in two specific places. First, the *implementation substrate* is constrained: the cortical learned spatial model is read as *necessarily* phase-coupled-oscillator across [[cortical-column|cortical columns]], because the [[cortical-traveling-waves|cortical traveling waves]] and [[neural-phase-pattern-propagation|phase-pattern propagation]] mechanisms that the cluster pages document are what allow column-local spatial fragments to compose into a whole-system estimate without a central blackboard. Second, the *[[consciousness]]-side* role is asserted: the [[three-tier-spatial-consciousness-model|three-tier-spatial-consciousness-model]] treats the learned spatial model as one of the *constitutive* layers of phenomenal "where I am" experience, which is a stronger claim than the cognitive-map literature usually defends. Both commitments yield testable predictions: the first about cross-column phase-locking signatures during navigation tasks, the second about the relationship between spatial-disorientation phenomena and phase-coordination breakdown.
